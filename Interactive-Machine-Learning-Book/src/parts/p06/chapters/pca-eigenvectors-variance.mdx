## PCA chooses an orthonormal coordinate system

For centered row-batch Xc and covariance C=XcᵀXc/q, a unit column direction v produces projected scalar scores z=Xcv. Their variance under the same divisor q is

$$
\frac{\|X_cv\|_2^2}{q}=v^TCv,\qquad \|v\|_2=1.
$$

The unit constraint is essential: multiplying a direction by ten multiplies its score variance by a hundred without finding a better line. PCA maximizes this variance over unit directions. It then repeats inside orthogonal complements, so later components do not duplicate earlier directions.

For real symmetric C, an orthonormal eigenbasis exists:

$$
Cv_j=\lambda_jv_j,\quad V^TV=I,\quad
\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d\ge0.
$$

V has d×d shape, with directions in its columns. Covariance is positive semidefinite because every directional variance is nonnegative. These directions describe variation in the supplied metric, not class boundaries, causes or supervised feature importance. PCA needs no target labels.

## Derive why the maximum is an eigenvector

Use a Lagrange multiplier for the unit constraint:

$$
\mathcal L(v,\lambda)=v^TCv-\lambda(v^Tv-1),\qquad
\nabla_v\mathcal L=2Cv-2\lambda v=0.
$$

At a stationary direction, Cv=λv, and multiplying by vᵀ shows its variance is λ. To find the maximum, write a unit vector in the orthonormal eigenbasis: v=Va with aᵀa=1. Then

$$
v^TCv=\sum_{j=1}^d\lambda_j a_j^2\le\lambda_1\sum_j a_j^2=\lambda_1.
$$

The bound is attained by a leading eigenvector. Restricting to vectors orthogonal to v1 removes its coordinate and leaves λ2 as the next maximum. Distinct eigenvalues determine distinct lines, up to sign. If the largest eigenvalue repeats, any unit vector in its eigenspace attains the same maximum.

## Work the original eigenpairs literally

For the sample covariance from III-29,

$$
C=\begin{bmatrix}10/3&8/3\\8/3&10/3\end{bmatrix},\qquad
v_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad
v_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Multiplying gives Cv1=6v1 and Cv2=(2/3)v2. Their dot product is zero and each norm is one. The eigenvalue sum is 6+2/3=20/3, equal to the covariance trace. Population normalization multiplies both eigenvalues by 3/4: 4.5 and 0.5. The directions are unchanged.

Define explained-variance fractions only when the total is positive:

$$
r_j=\frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_h\lambda_h},\qquad
R_k=\sum_{j=1}^k r_j.
$$

Here r1=6/(20/3)=0.9 and r2=0.1. One component retains 90% of the empirical feature variation; it does not give 90% prediction accuracy. Zero components retain zero when there is positive spread; all components retain one. For a zero matrix, the denominator is zero and every ratio is undefined.

## Signs and repeated eigenvalues are not bugs

If Cv=λv, then C(−v)=λ(−v). Flipping a component flips its scores but leaves the reconstructed points unchanged because both factors change sign. The browser chooses a reproducible display sign for the leading eigenvector and obtains the perpendicular second vector. Another library may choose different signs; raw vector equality is the wrong parity test. Compare the projector vvᵀ or the absolute inner product for a simple eigenvalue.

For C=αI, all directions have the same variance. A one-component reconstruction depends on the chosen line, but its total discarded energy is the same for every line. More generally, if a repeated eigenspace straddles the retained/discarded boundary, there is no unique rank-k projector. If you retain an entire repeated eigenspace, its projector is unique even though the basis within it can rotate. Our oracle compares orthogonality, eigen relations and optimal SSE in the ambiguous case, without requiring NumPy to return the browser's basis.

Near a repeated spectrum, a tiny perturbation can rotate the vectors substantially. The lab warns when the computed gap is within 64 machine epsilons of total variance. This is a conditioning warning; it does not merge eigenvalues, choose a unique axis, or zero out variation. Exact computed equality is labeled separately.

## Calculate the small eigenvalue without subtracting large equals

For a symmetric two-by-two covariance $C=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{bmatrix}$, the formal eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac{a+c}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{a-c}{2}\right)^2+b^2}.
$$

The plus expression is well behaved after scaling by the largest entry magnitude and using `hypot`. The minus expression can lose all small positive variation through cancellation. For diagonal C=diag(0.5,5×10⁻¹⁷), both large terms can round to 0.25, making their difference zero.

This kernel uses the scaled plus formula to obtain a stable leading direction, choosing the longer of two algebraic eigenvector formulas. It obtains the minor eigenvalue from centered data directly:

$$
\lambda_2=\frac1q\sum_i (x_{c,i}^Tv_2)^2.
$$

This sums nonnegative small quantities instead of subtracting two large quantities. No tolerance clamps a small positive value to zero. Last-bit ordering is restored if directional energy rounds above the computed leading value in a nearly repeated spectrum. Input dimensions, IDs and all finite arithmetic are checked before any zero-spread return.
