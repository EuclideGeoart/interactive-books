## Ask what a line can express

The opening XOR observations have positive labels at [−1,−1] and [1,1], negative labels at [−1,1] and [1,−1]. Their convex hulls intersect, so a hard input-space line is impossible. Switch to **linear**: the soft optimum can account for disagreement, but cannot invent nonlinear expressiveness. With the symmetric cloud its normal can be zero, giving ties rather than a separating line.

Switch to **polynomial**, degree 2, gamma 1 and offset 0. A product of coordinates distinguishes the diagonals. Switch to **RBF** and alter gamma. The similarity scale changes, the optimizer refits, and the resulting curved zero contour changes accordingly. The laboratory never puts a decorative curve over an unrelated linear model.

## Define a valid similarity

A kernel represents an inner product in a feature space:

$$
K(x,z)=\langle\phi(x),\phi(z)\rangle_{\mathcal H}.
$$

For a finite sample, the Gram matrix has n×n shape with Kij=K(xi,xj). It must be symmetric positive semidefinite: for every real column c, cᵀKc≥0. Symmetry alone is insufficient, and kernel entries need not be probabilities or individually nonnegative. PSD is required for the dual's concavity and for interpreting its quadratic form as a squared feature-space norm.

The laboratory restricts parameters to known valid kernel families; it does not accept an arbitrary user-supplied similarity matrix. The independent oracle checks Gram eigenvalues with NumPy `eigvalsh` and separately checks literal entries. This tests the implementation; finite-sample PSD alone does not prove a proposed kernel is valid for every possible dataset.

## Three kernel families and bounded parameters

The linear kernel K(x,z)=xᵀz gives the original input-space model. The polynomial kernel is

$$
K(x,z)=(\gamma x^Tz+c_0)^d,
\quad\gamma>0,\ c_0\ge0,\ d\in\{1,2,3,4\}.
$$

Nonnegative coefficients and an integral degree make this a valid polynomial inner product. The laboratory bounds gamma to [0.05,2] and offset to [0,2]. Degree must be integral; a fractional exponent is rejected even if one particular base would give a finite result. Offset zero gives a homogeneous polynomial; a positive offset permits lower-degree terms.

The radial basis kernel is

$$
K(x,z)=\exp(-\gamma\|x-z\|^2),\qquad\gamma\in[0.05,2].
$$

It gives one on the diagonal and decreases with separation. Increasing gamma makes similarity more local. It is not a Gaussian probability density: there is no normalization constant, and Gram rows do not sum to one. RBF feature space is implicit and generally infinite-dimensional. The code computes finite similarities and finite kernel sums; it does **not** materialize infinite coordinate vectors.

## Derive the kernel decision from the dual

The representer form of the fitted feature-space normal is w=Σαi yi φ(xi). Thus the same bounded soft dual uses only K, and prediction is

$$
f(x)=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i K(x_i,x)+b,\quad
\hat y=\operatorname{sign}f(x),\quad y^T\alpha=0,\quad0\le\alpha_i\le C.
$$

The α column has n×1 shape. X still stores n×2 source rows, but the implicit feature-space normal can have a different or infinite dimension. There is no source-space weight vector for a nonlinear fit. Its squared norm is

$$
\|w\|_{\mathcal H}^2=(\alpha\odot y)^TK(\alpha\odot y).
$$

The regularizer and primal–dual diagnostics use this feature norm. Dividing f(x) by the feature norm, f(x)/‖w‖𝓗 (the square root of the displayed norm squared), gives a feature-space signed distance when the normal is nonzero, but generally **not** Euclidean distance from x to a curved input-space boundary. The laboratory deliberately leaves source-space signed distance unavailable for nonlinear kernels. Its f=±1 contours represent functional margin levels; their visual spacing in the input plane need not be constant.

Positive α means a support coefficient, while $0<\alpha<C$ and α=C have the KKT interpretations from III-33. Location alone does not determine a coefficient. On-margin flags remain separate and the actual Gram matrix stays visible even if fitting diagnostics fail.

## Work a polynomial feature map literally

For degree 2, gamma 1, offset 0 in two dimensions,

$$
\phi(x)=\begin{bmatrix}x_1^2\\\sqrt2 x_1x_2\\x_2^2\end{bmatrix},\quad
\phi(x)^T\phi(z)=(x^Tz)^2.
$$

For the XOR rows in opening order, within-class diagonally opposite vectors have dot product −2; self dot products are 2; cross-class adjacent vectors have dot product zero. Squaring gives

$$
K=\begin{bmatrix}4&4&0&0\\4&4&0&0\\0&0&4&4\\0&0&4&4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For C≥1/8, one symmetric optimum has all α=1/8 and b=0, yielding f(x)=x1x2, feature norm squared 1/2 and primal=dual=1/4. The feature normal is [0,1/√2,0]ᵀ. Its zero set consists of both coordinate axes in the input plane. Coefficients are not unique because some feature rows coincide; a deterministic pair solver may place the same total class mass on fewer observations. Compare reconstructed decisions and objective rather than demanding these particular coefficients from every solver.

For x=[1,0], z=[0,1], the linear entry is zero; the degree-2 polynomial with offset 1 is one; RBF gamma 1 is exp(−2). Diagonal RBF entries are one. These are hand-defined expectations, not outputs generated by the browser.

For the XOR RBF kernel, write a=exp(−4), so cross-class entries are a and opposite same-class entries a². A symmetric optimum with inactive upper bounds has α=1/(1−a)², b=0 and primal=dual=2/(1−a)². C=4 is above that coefficient. This is a useful analytic anchor for the owner to compare against the actual optimizer.

## Common mistakes and numerical limits

A nonlinear score is not an input-space distance or calibrated probability. More localized RBF similarity can fit small-scale training patterns and still generalize poorly. Large polynomial entries can worsen conditioning even with valid bounded parameters. PSD roundoff is diagnostic: significantly negative pair curvature is rejected; a nonpositive curvature within the fixed 10⁻¹⁰ numerical allowance uses feasible endpoints and still must pass global KKT/gap diagnostics.

<Callout title="A fit can be unavailable" kind="numerical">All iterative solutions are approximate. Bounded parameters do not guarantee convergence within 1200 steps, especially near degeneracy or ill-conditioning. A failed diagnostic removes the fitted contour and explains the result. Nonfinite or nonzero-underflow arithmetic is rejected; neither zeroing a norm nor widening tolerances repairs an unresolved model.</Callout>

## Connections and summary

Dot products, PSD matrices and convex duality explain the kernel trick. You can read Gram entries, reconstruct decisions and distinguish feature-space norms from source-space distance. The model family, C and gamma remain hyperparameters: III-35 will explain how to select such settings using validation evidence rather than training fit alone.
