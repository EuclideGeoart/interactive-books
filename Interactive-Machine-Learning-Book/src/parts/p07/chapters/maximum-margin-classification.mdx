## Start with the gap, not a guessed line

Drag point b toward the positive class. The line is recomputed from the observations: its normal w, intercept b, constraints and distances change together. The point ID b and the intercept b are different objects; the ledger labels each explicitly. Select **anchor data** for the simplest exact example. Select **overlap data** or **coincident data** to see a failed assumption: opposite classes cannot always be separated.

Many lines can separate two clouds. Maximum-margin classification chooses the one with the largest clearance to the nearest labeled observations. Clearance measures robustness to movement in the supplied feature metric; it is not a probability, a guarantee of future accuracy, or a claim about causal importance. Scaling a feature changes that metric and can change the answer.

## Define the model and its shapes

A sample x and weight w are mathematical 2×1 columns. The laboratory stores n samples as rows of X, an n×2 matrix, and labels y as an n×1 column with entries −1 and +1. The scalar intercept is separate from w:

$$
f(x)=w^Tx+b,\qquad f(X)=Xw+\mathbf1b.
$$

Positive scores predict +1, negative scores −1. Exactly zero is an explicit tie, recorded as decision 0. The zero set is the model boundary. For nonzero w, it is a line perpendicular to w. We do not penalize the intercept. Training accuracy counts correct signs; the optimization objective controls margin geometry instead.

The functional margin of observation i is yi f(xi). It has no fixed unit: multiplying w and b by a positive constant preserves the boundary and predictions while multiplying functional margins. Fix the model's scale by requiring the nearest functional margins to equal one. The geometric signed distance to the boundary is f(x)/‖w‖, and the label-adjusted geometric margin is yi f(xi)/‖w‖.

## Derive the maximum-margin objective

The parallel lines f=−1 and f=+1 are each 1/‖w‖ away from f=0. Their total separation is 2/‖w‖. Maximizing this gap is equivalent to minimizing the squared normal length under the normalized constraints:

$$
\min_{w,b}\frac12\|w\|_2^2
\quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(w^Tx_i+b)\ge1\quad\text{for every }i.
$$

The factor 1/2 simplifies derivatives. It is not a mean over samples. A solution needs strict linear separability before scale normalization; overlapping class convex hulls make the constraints infeasible. Opposite labels at the same coordinate directly require f(x)≥1 and f(x)≤−1 at once. No finite w,b can satisfy both.

Introduce nonnegative multipliers αi and form the Lagrangian. Stationarity with respect to w and the unpenalized intercept gives

$$
w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i x_i,\qquad\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0.
$$

Substitution gives the concave dual:

$$
\max_{\alpha\ge0,\ y^T\alpha=0}
\sum_i\alpha_i-\frac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_jx_i^Tx_j.
$$

Complementarity is αi(yi f(xi)−1)=0. A positive multiplier requires an active margin constraint at an optimum. The converse need not hold: duplicate or redundant active constraints can have zero multiplier in one optimal representation. The ring shows $\alpha>10^{-9}$ by the solver's diagnostic convention; the ledger's on-margin flag independently tests |yi f−1|≤2×10⁻⁵. These numerical classifications do not redefine mathematical support.

## Work the two-point optimum by hand

Take negative x−=[−1,0]ᵀ and positive x+=[1,0]ᵀ. The constraints are w1−b≥1 and w1+b≥1. Adding gives w1≥1. Choosing w2=0 minimizes the norm; w1=1 forces b=0.

$$
w=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad b=0,\quad
\gamma=1,\quad\alpha_-=\alpha_+=\frac12.
$$

The scores are −1 and +1, signed distances −1 and +1, and both label-adjusted margins are one. The half-margin is one and full gap two. The primal is 1/2. The dual is α−+α+−‖w‖²/2=1−1/2=1/2. Equality holds because −α−+α+=0. This is an independently hand-derived fixture, not output copied from the program.

If the samples move to [−2,0] and [2,0], w=[1/2,0], b=0, half-margin two, and each α=1/8. Coordinate scale and geometric margin change, while functional margins remain one under normalization.

## How the bounded laboratory solves hard margins

The implementation first builds the two class convex hulls in the input plane. If they intersect, including crossing edges, containment or coincident opposite points, it reports infeasibility and draws no fitted model. If they are disjoint, let n and p be their closest points, with δ=p−n. Supporting planes perpendicular to δ touch those hulls, so

$$
w=\frac{2\delta}{\|\delta\|^2},\qquad
b=-w^T\frac{p+n}{2},\qquad\gamma=\frac{\|\delta\|}{2}.
$$

Endpoint-to-segment projections enumerate the shortest hull distance. Barycentric weights of the closest segment points distribute the common dual mass 2/‖δ‖² among their observations. This gives an explicit dual certificate; it is not merely a visually plausible separator. The result reconstructs all training scores, constraints, primal and dual values and checks KKT, equality, nonnegative coefficients and gap before allowing a line to be displayed.

The API is intentionally two-dimensional and limited to 2–12 rows with both labels and coordinates in [−5,5]. This geometric method does not extend automatically to higher dimensions. Orientation signs use an exact dyadic fallback on the original binary64 coordinates. Closest-point projections and fitted coefficients still round; even an exactly separable configuration can yield numerical unavailability or diagnostic failure. A distance that cannot be squared or inverted representably is unavailable. No failed hard solution is displayed as fitted.

## Common mistakes and limits

A large raw f(x) is not a large geometric distance unless you divide by the normal length. A point on f=0 is a decision tie, not a support margin point. A ring is a coefficient property, not simply a location on a drawn line. Maximizing training clearance alone does not establish generalization. Outliers can dominate hard separation, and one conflicting label can destroy feasibility.
