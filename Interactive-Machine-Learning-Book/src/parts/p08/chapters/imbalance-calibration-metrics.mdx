## Imbalance makes the question matter

With 99 negatives and one positive, predicting every row negative gives 99% accuracy and zero positive recall. That can be unusable when missing the positive is costly. Accuracy asks the fraction of all decisions that are correct; it does not express the cost of each error or ensure that both classes are served. Class prevalence changes its meaning.

Define positive by p≥τ. True positives TP have y=1 and a positive decision; false positives FP have y=0 and a positive decision; true negatives TN have y=0 and a negative decision; false negatives FN have y=1 and a negative decision. These are counts of observations, not probabilities.

$$
\mathrm{accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{n},\quad
\mathrm{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad
\mathrm{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},
$$

$$
\mathrm{balanced\ accuracy}=\frac12\left(\frac{TP}{TP+FN}+\frac{TN}{TN+FP}\right),\quad
F_1=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}.
$$

A zero denominator means undefined. F1's count formula remains defined as zero when there are actual positives but no predicted positives. If TP=FP=FN=0, its denominator is zero and F1 is undefined. Balanced accuracy needs both classes present. Declaring these conventions prevents silent disagreements between libraries with different zero-division options.

## Count the original forecasts by hand

The six negative forecasts are 0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4,0.6,0.8; the two positive forecasts are 0.5,0.9. At τ=0.5, TP=2, FP=2, TN=4, FN=0. Accuracy is 6/8=3/4, precision is 2/4=1/2, recall is 1 and F1 is 4/6=2/3. Negative recall is 4/6=2/3, so balanced accuracy is (1+2/3)/2=5/6.

At τ=0.8, equality keeps the negative forecast 0.8 positive. TP=1, FP=1, TN=5, FN=1; precision and recall are both 1/2. The board lists all distinct observed cutoffs plus 0 and 1. Between neighboring cutoffs, the confusion counts stay fixed. Increasing a threshold removes predicted positives and cannot increase recall on this fixed dataset, though precision need not change monotonically.

A PR tradeoff is relevant when positives are scarce: it explicitly compares positive recovery with the fraction of positive alerts that are correct. Choose a threshold using development data and operational costs, then lock it before the final test. The metric board is descriptive; it does not silently tune thresholds on the regression test group.

## Calibration asks a probability question

For a calibrated forecaster, among observations assigned probability near q, the positive fraction should be near q. Good ranking and threshold accuracy do not establish that property. A forecast of 0.99 and a forecast of 0.51 produce the same positive decision at τ=0.5, yet claim very different confidence.

The Brier score measures probability squared error:

$$
B=\frac1n\sum_i(p_i-y_i)^2.
$$

For the original rows, the squared errors are .01,.04,.09,.16,.36,.64,.25,.01. Their sum is 1.56=39/25 and B=39/200=0.195. It is a probability error score, influenced by more than calibration alone, and it does not depend on τ. A lower Brier score can reflect improved discrimination as well as calibration; the reliability diagram provides a different descriptive view.

Natural-log loss is

$$
L=-\frac1n\sum_i[y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)].
$$

The code evaluates only the probability of the true class, avoiding 0·log 0. A true-class probability of zero gives unbounded log loss. It is represented as unavailable with an explicit reason, never clipped to a convenient finite number.

## Fixed bins, explicit membership

The five reliability bins are [0,.2), [.2,.4), [.4,.6), [.6,.8), [.8,1]. Boundary values enter the bin on their right; 1 enters the last bin. For each nonempty bin, plot its mean forecast against its empirical positive fraction. Original counts are 1,2,2,1,2. The [.4,.6) bin contains .4 with label 0 and .5 with label 1: mean forecast .45, frequency .5. The final bin contains .8 with label 0 and .9 with label 1: mean .85, frequency .5.

Tiny bins are noisy. Empty bins have no defined mean or frequency and are omitted from the plot. Fixed bins keep comparisons interpretable; changing bins after inspecting results can hide discrepancies. These eight artificial forecasts cannot certify calibration on a deployment population.

<Callout title="A metric is a declared decision" kind="misconception">
Do not choose accuracy solely because it is familiar, or claim a calibration improvement because accuracy rose. Start with error costs, prevalence, probability use and the evaluation population. Rebalancing training data can change learned probabilities and does not by itself calibrate forecasts to deployment prevalence.
</Callout>
