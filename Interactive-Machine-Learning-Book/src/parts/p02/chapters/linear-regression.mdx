## A prediction is a claim about an observation

An observation supplies an input $x_i$ and a target $y_i$. An affine predictor uses two shared parameters to produce a number:

$$
\hat y_i = wx_i+b.
$$

The weight $w$ controls how much the prediction changes when the input increases by one. The bias $b$ is the prediction at $x=0$. Changing either parameter changes the same line for every sample. Moving an observation changes the data; it does not directly move the predictor's parameters.

Select a sample in the stage. Its filled circle is the observed pair $(x_i,y_i)$, and its hollow diamond is $(x_i,\hat y_i)$. The dashed segment is vertical because this loss compares target and prediction at the **same input**. It does not measure the shortest distance to the line.

Our residual convention is

$$
r_i=\hat y_i-y_i.
$$

A positive residual means overprediction; a negative residual means underprediction. The sign is useful even though the squared error $r_i^2$ is nonnegative. Some books reverse the residual convention, so check the definition before copying a gradient.

## Samples, columns and shapes

These five unitless observations were explicitly hand-authored for this experiment: inputs $[-2,-1,0,1,2]$ and targets $[-3,-1,1,3,4]$, with stable IDs `s1` through `s5`. They are synthetic toy data, not measurements or evidence about a population. The last target is one below the otherwise exact line $y=2x+1$.

Mathematical vectors are columns. A batch puts one observation in each row of $X$:

$$
X=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\-1\\0\\1\\2\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{5\times1},
\quad W=\begin{bmatrix}w\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{1\times1},
\quad y=\begin{bmatrix}-3\\-1\\1\\3\\4\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{5\times1}.
$$

The scalar bias broadcasts to all five entries: $\hat y=XW+b\mathbf1$, where $\mathbf1$ is a $5\times1$ column of ones. Thus predictions and residuals also have shape $5\times1$. There is no intercept column in this chapter's $X$; the bias remains a separate scalar. For $n$ observations, replace five with $n$.

The inspector reports these actual shapes. Stable IDs identify observations when their coordinates change; their IDs are not extra features.

## Turn all residuals into one objective

Mean squared error averages the squared vertical residuals:

$$
L(w,b)=\operatorname{MSE}(w,b)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(wx_i+b-y_i)^2.
$$

Squaring prevents positive and negative errors from cancelling and makes larger residuals contribute more. Averaging distinguishes MSE from the sum of squared errors. This definition contains **no factor of one half** and no square root.

MSE is the training objective in this experiment. A low value says the predictor matches these targets; it does not establish causal influence, reliable extrapolation, or performance on unseen observations. We have no separate validation or test set here.

| s5 | 2 | 4 | 1 | -3 | 9 |

The squared errors sum to $20.5$, giving $L=20.5/5=4.1$. Their signed residuals sum to $-4$; the weighted residuals sum to

$$
\sum_i r_i x_i=2(-2)+0.5(-1)+(-1)(0)+(-2.5)(1)+(-3)(2)=-13.
$$

The gradients below therefore give $dw=(2/5)(-13)=-5.2$ and $db=(2/5)(-4)=-1.6$. Subtract both gradients, evaluated at the **same old state**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_{\mathrm{next}}&=0.5-0.1(-5.2)=1.02,\\
b_{\mathrm{next}}&=0-0.1(-1.6)=0.16.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first decrease is an observation about this update, not a promise that every permitted learning rate decreases loss.

## Derive the analytical gradients

Hold the data fixed. The chain rule and the partial derivatives of the affine predictor give

$$
\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial w}=x_i,
\qquad
\frac{\partial r_i}{\partial b}=1.
$$

For a squared residual, the outer derivative contributes $2r_i$. Differentiating the average term by term gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}&=\frac2n\sum_i r_i x_i=dw,\\
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}&=\frac2n\sum_i r_i=db.
\end{aligned}
$$

The gradient is the column $\nabla L=[dw,db]^{\mathsf T}$. The weight gradient includes the input because changing $w$ affects each prediction by $x_i$ times that change. The bias gradient weights every sample equally. For the observation at $x=0$, the weight's contribution is zero even if its residual is large.

Gradient descent takes the simultaneous update

$$
\begin{bmatrix}w_{\mathrm{next}}\\b_{\mathrm{next}}\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}w\\b\end{bmatrix}
-\eta\begin{bmatrix}dw\\db\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The implementation calculates these analytical derivatives directly. It does not use automatic differentiation or a fitted-model library. A central finite difference, $[L(w+h,b)-L(w-h,b)]/(2h)$, is a separate numerical check of $dw$; it is not the update rule used by the stage.

## Counterexamples clarify the limits

**Constant inputs are still valid predictor data.** If two samples have $x=2$ and targets $1$ and $3$, the predictors $(w,b)=(0,2)$ and $(1,0)$ both predict $2$ for both observations. Both have MSE $1$. The data constrain $2w+b$, so they do not identify a unique weight and bias. A single observation has the same kind of ambiguity. Neither case makes prediction, residuals or gradients undefined.

**A perfect fit has zero gradient.** For observations $(-1,-1)$ and $(1,3)$ with $w=2$, $b=1$, both residuals are zero. MSE and both gradients are zero, so an update leaves the parameters unchanged.

**A valid rate can overshoot.** With the single observation $(2,1)$, start at $w=b=0$ and $\eta=1$. MSE is initially $1$, and gradients are $dw=-4$, $db=-2$. The next parameters $(4,2)$ predict $10$, so the next MSE is $(10-1)^2=81$. A positive bounded rate alone is not a convergence certificate. Input scale and curvature matter.

## Numerical considerations

State uses binary64 numbers. Given sample coordinates use full round-trip decimal text in the table and accessible names. Computed predictions, residuals, squared errors, MSE and update values are displayed to four decimal places and marked approximately. A displayed approximate zero can therefore hide a small nonzero loss or gradient. Display rounding never changes the values used for an update. Keyboard movement adds its increment to the existing coordinate with ordinary binary64 arithmetic, preserving fine precision without extra decimal truncation; pointer movement explicitly snaps to the stated 0.1 lattice. Python/TypeScript parity is checked with absolute and relative tolerances of $10^{-12}$, rather than exact equality between independently rounded calculations.

The numerical API requires 1–64 observations, unique nonempty IDs, finite inputs and $0<\eta\leq1$. Finite inputs can still overflow during products, squared residuals or reductions. Such arithmetic is rejected with an informative error; an overflowing replay returns no partial history. Subnormal values and underflow follow binary64 arithmetic, and a very small update can round to no parameter change.

The plot has equal x/y scale and a fixed domain from −6 to 6. Predictions outside that plane are clipped visually but remain visible numerically in the table. Pointer movement stops at the observation editor's boundary; numeric entry instead rejects an out-of-range value. Large touch targets expand with the rendered plot size, and only sample handles capture touch gestures, so the rest of the page remains scrollable.
