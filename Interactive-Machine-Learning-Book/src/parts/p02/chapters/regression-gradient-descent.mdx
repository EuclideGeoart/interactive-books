## Separate objective from solver

The training objective remains

$$
L(w,b)=\frac1n\sum_i(wx_i+b-y_i)^2.
$$

The analytical gradients are $dw=(2/n)\sum_i r_ix_i$ and $db=(2/n)\sum_i r_i$. Gradient descent uses $w^+=w-\eta dw$, $b^+=b-\eta db$, with both derivatives evaluated at the old state. The learning rate is a solver setting. Changing it does not change the objective or its optimum.

The gradient points toward the steepest local increase. Its negative gives a descent direction for a sufficiently small step. A finite step can overshoot. “The gradient is correct” and “this update is useful” are separate checks.

## Derive the curvature test

Write $\theta=[w,b]^\mathsf T$ and $A=[x,\mathbf1]$. The Hessian is constant:

$$
H=\frac2nA^\mathsf TA
=2\begin{bmatrix}\overline{x^2}&\bar x\\\bar x&1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For an identifiable optimum $\theta_*$, the error evolves as $e^+=(I-\eta H)e$. Along an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$, its scalar component is multiplied by $1-\eta\lambda$. Thus every positive-curvature component contracts when

$$
0<\eta<\frac2{\lambda_{\max}}.
$$

A negative multiplier gives alternating sides. Magnitude below one means damping; magnitude one gives persistent oscillation; magnitude above one diverges if that mode is excited. A component already exactly zero stays zero, even at an unstable rate. In a rank-deficient problem a zero-curvature direction retains its initial component, so prediction convergence does not identify a unique parameter column.

## Work the canonical update and surface

For the five observations, $\bar x=0$ and $\overline{x^2}=2$, so $H=\operatorname{diag}(4,2)$. The optimum is $(w_*,b_*)=(1.8,0.8)$ and

$$
L(w,b)=0.08+2(w-1.8)^2+(b-0.8)^2.
$$

The parameter plot draws actual MSE contours at $L_*+0.2$, $L_*+1$ and $L_*+4$, not decorative ellipses. It projects the current point, optimum and prospective next point from the same numerical state. Horizontal increments change $w$ and vertical increments change $b$; the parameter plane has equal coordinate scales. Its axis domain expands during divergence.

At $w=0.5,b=0$, gradients are $(-5.2,-1.6)$ and MSE is $4.1$. Rate $0.1$ produces $(1.02,0.16)$ and MSE $1.7064$. Fast-mode error multiplies by $1-0.1(4)=0.6$; bias error multiplies by $0.8$. At rate $0.4$ the fast-mode multiplier is $-0.6$ and the bias multiplier is $0.2$. At $0.75$ they are $-2$ and $-0.5$: the slope error doubles in magnitude each step.

The critical rate is $0.5$. Exactly at it the slope oscillation is undamped; a decreasing bias error does not make the whole parameter column converge. Observe loss and parameter behavior together.

## Mistakes, stopping and numerical limits

Do not update the bias using a newly updated weight; that is a different algorithm. Do not drop the derivative's factor of two while retaining full MSE. Do not infer convergence from one lower loss or a positive bounded learning rate.

Feature scale changes curvature. Doubling all inputs multiplies their squared contribution by four and usually requires a smaller rate. A slow mode can take many steps even while a fast mode is near its stability limit. In real fitting, inspect loss change, gradient norm, parameter change and an iteration budget; a display rounded to zero is not a convergence certificate.

The pure reference accepts $0<\eta\le1$ and rejects arithmetic overflow. The interactive rate is $0.001\ldots1$ and displayed coefficients are bounded to ±1000000. A replay validates every prospective frame before installing any history. An unsupported step leaves the previous state visible with a reason; clipping a divergent weight would conceal the behavior being taught.
