## The objective and its stationary conditions

For $n$ scalar inputs, choose an affine predictor and squared vertical residuals:

$$
\hat y_i=wx_i+b,\quad r_i=\hat y_i-y_i,\quad L(w,b)=\frac1n\sum_i r_i^2.
$$

This is an optimization objective, not a procedure for finding it. “Least squares” describes what is minimized. A closed-form calculation and gradient descent can solve the same objective. Neither gives a guarantee of generalization to an unseen population.

Differentiating while holding the observations fixed gives

$$
\partial_bL=\frac2n\sum_i(wx_i+b-y_i)=0,
\qquad \partial_wL=\frac2n\sum_i x_i(wx_i+b-y_i)=0.
$$

The first equation implies $b=\bar y-w\bar x$. Substitute it into the second and group centered quantities:

$$
S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2,\quad S_{xy}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y),
\qquad w_*S_{xx}=S_{xy}.
$$

When $S_{xx}>0$, the unique result is $w_*=S_{xy}/S_{xx}$ and $b_*=\bar y-w_*\bar x$. Centering removes the intercept from the slope calculation. Geometrically the optimal residual column is orthogonal to both the input column and the column of ones. It is a projection onto the span of those two columns.

## Work the five-row optimum

The original hand-authored inputs are $[-2,-1,0,1,2]$ and targets $[-3,-1,1,3,4]$. There is no random seed, measurement claim or held-out set in this chapter. Here $\bar x=0$, $\bar y=0.8$, $S_{xx}=10$ and $S_{xy}=18$. Thus $w_*=1.8$, $b_*=0.8$.

| x | Target | Optimal prediction | Residual |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| -2 | -3 | -2.8 | 0.2 |
| -1 | -1 | -1 | 0 |
| 0 | 1 | 0.8 | -0.2 |
| 1 | 3 | 2.6 | -0.4 |
| 2 | 4 | 4.4 | 0.4 |

Both $\sum r_i$ and $\sum x_ir_i$ are zero. The squared residual sum is $0.4$, so MSE is $0.4/5=0.08$. A positive optimal training error is expected because the last observation departs from the otherwise exact line $2x+1$.

The active design in this sheet is augmented: $X=[\mathbf1,x]\in\mathbb R^{n\times2}$, $W=[b,w]^\mathsf T\in\mathbb R^{2\times1}$ and $y\in\mathbb R^{n\times1}$. This intercept-first notation is equivalent to III-07's separate scalar bias; it does not alter its predictor or MSE normalization.

## Zero variance is an identification failure

If every input equals $c$, only the combination $wc+b$ is constrained. Its best value is $\bar y$. Every pair satisfying $wc+b=\bar y$ is a minimizer. We choose $w=0,b=\bar y$ as an explicitly labeled horizontal representative, **not** as a unique slope or the minimum norm parameter pair.

For two observations $(2,1)$ and $(2,3)$, both $(w,b)=(0,2)$ and $(1,0)$ predict $2$ and have MSE $1$. A single observation has the same ambiguity. Do not divide $0/0$, invent an identifiable slope, or reject an otherwise meaningful prediction problem.

When every input equals $x_0$, the plot draws the chosen predictor over $[x_0-1,x_0+1]$ so the line remains visible beyond the overlapping prediction markers. These are points on the displayed function, not additional observations or evidence identifying a slope. With a nonzero observed span, the drawing extends $0.15$ beyond each observed endpoint.

## Explore the assumptions

Fit, then edit one target without fitting again. The current line and current MSE reflect your displayed coefficients; “optimal data MSE” belongs to the newly computed optimum. Press fit to adopt it. Editing the data and optimizing the coefficients are different actions.

Change only one input away from a constant-input dataset and refit. The slope becomes identifiable, but a tiny input spread can make it extremely sensitive to target errors. A unique answer can still be poorly conditioned. Extrapolating far beyond the observed inputs magnifies that sensitivity.

The sheet bounds editable observations to −5…5; its pure kernel validates broader finite inputs.
</Callout>
