## Intuition: no break where you look

A continuous function can be drawn through the point $x=a$ without lifting the pen. That picture needs three separate things: there must be a point to draw, the curve must arrive at one height from both sides, and that height must be the point itself. Each can fail on its own, and the explorer lets you break each one.

## Definition

A function $f$ is **continuous at $a$** when

1. $f(a)$ is defined;
2. $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists (both one-sided limits exist, are finite and agree);
3. $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$.

If $a$ is an endpoint of the domain, only the side inside the domain is used: $f$ is continuous from the right at $a$ when $\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a)$. A function is continuous **on an interval** when it is continuous at every point of it.

## Mechanics: what the explorer checks

The opening function is $x^2+c$ for $x<1$ and $m\,x$ for $x>1$, with $f(1)$ taken from the left piece, the right piece, a custom value $v$, or left undefined. The inspector shows the three conditions as yes/no rows derived from the exact one-sided limits and the exact value; the verdict names the kind of discontinuity. The global row lists every discontinuity inside the view, so you can compare a statement at one point with a statement about the whole function.

## Derivation: when the pieces join

At $x=1$ the left piece approaches $1^2+c=1+c$ and the right piece approaches $m\cdot1=m$. Condition 2 holds exactly when

$$
1+c=m.
$$

Condition 3 then requires $f(1)$ to equal this common value. With the right-piece value $f(1)=m$, continuity holds exactly when $c=m-1$. With a custom value $v$, it also needs $v=m$.

## Worked example

Take $c=1$, $m=2$ and set $f(1)=0$. The left limit is $1+1=2$ and the right limit is $2$, so the two-sided limit is $2$. But $f(1)=0\ne2$: condition 3 fails. This is a **removable** discontinuity: redefining $f(1)=2$ repairs it without changing any other value.

## Kinds of discontinuity

* **Removable:** the limit exists but $f(a)$ is missing or different. Redefine one value to repair it.
* **Jump:** both one-sided limits exist but differ. No single value can repair it.
* **Infinite:** at least one side is unbounded, as for $1/(x-1)$ at $1$.
* **Oscillating:** no one-sided limit exists, as for $\sin(1/x)$ at $0$ (III.1).

At an **endpoint**, such as $\sqrt x$ at $0$, the missing left side is not a failure: $\sqrt x$ is continuous from the right there.

## Common mistakes

A graph that "looks connected" at screen resolution can hide a missing or moved point. Continuity is a property at a point; one break does not make a function discontinuous everywhere, and one continuous point does not make it continuous everywhere. A function is not discontinuous at points outside its domain — it is simply not defined there; $1/x$ is continuous on its whole domain.

## Numerical considerations

A floating-point test such as `abs(f(a+h) - f(a)) < tol` cannot certify continuity: a jump smaller than the tolerance passes, and steep continuous functions fail. The explorer uses exact one-sided limits of rational pieces; for sampled data, continuity is a modelling assumption, not something measurements prove.

## Engineering and ML connections

Many algorithms assume continuity: bisection (finding a root between a sign change), interpolation, and gradient methods all rely on values that do not jump. Piecewise models — ReLU networks, decision thresholds, clipping — are continuous or discontinuous by design, and that choice affects whether small input changes can produce large output changes.
