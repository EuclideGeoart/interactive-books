When $x$ grows by $\Delta x$, the area gains a blue strip $v\,\Delta u$, a green strip $u\,\Delta v$ and a small corner $\Delta u\,\Delta v$. The inspector confirms that these three pieces add up **exactly** to the change of the product. Now choose **Quotient with v(x) = 0**: there is no value, no tangent and no rule result at that $x$.

## Intuition: a rate is a sensitivity

$u'(x)$ says how fast $u$ changes per unit of $x$. In a sum $u+v$, the two changes simply add. In a product, a change in $u$ matters more when the other factor $v$ is large: a 1-unit wider rectangle gains area equal to its height. Each factor's rate is weighted by the *other* factor's value.

## Definition

For differentiable $u$, $v$ and constants $\alpha$, $\beta$:

$$
(\alpha u+\beta v)'=\alpha u'+\beta v',
$$

$$
(uv)'=u'v+uv',
$$

and, wherever $v(x)\ne0$,

$$
\left(\frac uv\right)'=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}.
$$

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

The factors have rational coefficients, so every value is exact. The **rule** row applies the formula above. The **check** row reaches the same derivative independently: for sums and products by expanding the polynomial first and differentiating term by term; for a quotient $q=u/v$ by differentiating the identity $u=q\,v$ with the product rule. The agreement row is an exact rational comparison. Where $v(x)=0$, the quotient is undefined and every row says so.

## Derivation: the product rule from area

Let $\Delta u=u(x+\Delta x)-u(x)$ and $\Delta v=v(x+\Delta x)-v(x)$. Then exactly

$$
\Delta(uv)=v\,\Delta u+u\,\Delta v+\Delta u\,\Delta v .
$$

Divide by $\Delta x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}\frac{\Delta(uv)}{\Delta x}={}&v\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}+u\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta x}\\ &+\Delta u\,\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta x}.\end{aligned}
$$

As $\Delta x\to0$, the two quotients tend to $u'$ and $v'$, while $\Delta u\to0$ because a differentiable $u$ is continuous. The corner term disappears, leaving $u'v+uv'$.

**Quotient.** If $q=u/v$ and $v\ne0$, then $u=qv$, so $u'=q'v+qv'$ and

$$
q'=\frac{u'-qv'}{v}=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}.
$$

This derivation divides by $v$; it is meaningless where $v=0$.

## Worked example

Take $u=x+1$, $v=x^2+1$ at $x=1$. Then $u=2$, $v=2$, $u'=1$, $v'=2$.

* Product: $(uv)'=1\cdot2+2\cdot2=6$. Expanding first, $uv=x^3+x^2+x+1$ has derivative $3x^2+2x+1=6$.
* Quotient: $(u/v)'=(1\cdot2-2\cdot2)/2^2=-1/2$.

With $\Delta x=1/2$: $u$ grows to $2.5$ and $v$ to $3.25$. The area changes by $8.125-4=4.125$, which is $v\Delta u=1$ plus $u\Delta v=2.5$ plus $\Delta u\Delta v=0.625$.

## Domains and failures

A quotient rule result exists only where the quotient itself exists. For $v=x^2-1$ the quotient $u/v$ is undefined at $x=\pm1$. At $x=-1$ with $u=x+1$, cancelling $x+1$ removes a hole, but the derivative at $-1$ still does not exist in the original function, because the original function has no value there. The explorer draws dashed caution lines where $v$ vanishes.

## Common mistakes

$(uv)'\ne u'v'$: try $u=v=x$. In the quotient rule, the order $u'v-uv'$ matters. A rule result is not valid at points where the function is undefined, even if the formula's numerator looks harmless.

## Numerical considerations

These rules are exact identities; numerical issues appear only in evaluation. A quotient near a zero of $v$ divides by a tiny number, amplifying any error in $u$ or $v$; the derivative $u'v-uv'$ over $v^2$ amplifies even more. Exact arithmetic sidesteps this here; floating-point code should check how close $v$ is to zero.

## Engineering and ML connections

Automatic differentiation (III.10) applies exactly these local rules — sum, product, quotient — at every node of a computation. Normalisations such as softmax or layer normalisation contain quotients, and their derivatives inherit the same domain restrictions near a vanishing denominator.
