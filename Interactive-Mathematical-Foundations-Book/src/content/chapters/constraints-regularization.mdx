## Intuition: a boundary and a preference are different rules

A hard constraint declares some parameter points inadmissible. Projection repairs an update by choosing the nearest admissible point to its proposal. The proposal remains part of the explanation even when it is never used as the next optimizer state.

A penalty instead changes how expensive parameter points are. An L2 penalty makes large distance from the origin more costly, balancing that preference against the base objective. It does not reject a point merely because it lies outside a box or disk. A finite penalized minimum can remain outside either region.

Feasibility and progress are also different. Repeated projection can keep every point allowed while the trajectory oscillates. The canonical box example deliberately shows this: a boundary is a geometric rule, not an automatic convergence mechanism.

## Definition: the feasible set and the changed objective

Write $\theta=(x,y)$ and use the bounded quadratic family

$$
L(\theta)=\frac a2(x-c)^2+2y^2,\qquad
\nabla L=(a(x-c),4y),\qquad
H_L=\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\0&4\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The explorer has $1/4\leq a\leq4$ and $-2\leq c\leq2$. Its hard-region choices are no constraint, the fixed box $C=[-1,1]^2$, and the unit disk $C=\{\theta:\norm{\theta}_2\leq1\}$. For a closed convex region, Euclidean projection is

$$
P_C(z)=\argmin_{u\in C}\frac12\norm{u-z}_2^2.
$$

The separate penalty mechanism uses $0\leq\lambda\leq4$:

$$
J(\theta)=L(\theta)+\lambda(x^2+y^2),\qquad
\nabla J=\nabla L+2\lambda\theta,\qquad
H_J=H_L+2\lambda I.
$$

At $\lambda=0$, J equals L. When a penalty is active, its gradient supplies the proposal and both the base loss and penalized objective remain meaningful quantities to inspect. Choosing a hard region independently applies its projection to that proposal.

## Mechanics: record initialization and both parts of a step

For a requested initial point $\theta_{\mathrm{requested}}$, the feasible initial state is explicitly

$$
\theta_0=P_C(\theta_{\mathrm{requested}}),\qquad
r_0=\theta_0-\theta_{\mathrm{requested}}.
$$

Record the request, feasible start, and initialization correction before evaluating the first gradient. With no constraint, projection is the identity. With a box request $(2,-3)$, the feasible start is $(1,-1)$ and its correction is $(-1,2)$. This adjustment is initialization, not iteration 0's gradient step.

For each iteration, let g be the gradient of the selected objective and retain

$$
z_k=\theta_k-\eta g_k,\qquad
\theta_{k+1}=P_C(z_k),\qquad
q_k=\theta_{k+1}-z_k,\qquad
d_k=\theta_{k+1}-\theta_k.
$$

Here $q_k$ is the projection correction; $d_k$ is the actual parameter displacement. They satisfy $d_k=-\eta g_k+q_k$. Drawing the proposal vector as the actual displacement would hide the constraint's effect.

Reset restores $a=1,c=2$, requested start $(0,1)$, $\eta=1$, $K=12$, $\lambda=0$, and the box. Replay selects stored updates. Compare the unconstrained preset at $\eta=0.1$, the disk at the canonical rate, and the no-constraint penalty presets at $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=3$, both with rate $0.1$.

## Derivation: nearest points and the penalized minimum

The box distance objective separates into two coordinate squares. Each coordinate's nearest allowed value is its clamp:

$$
P_{\mathrm{box}}(z)=
\bigl(\min(1,\max(-1,z_x)),\min(1,\max(-1,z_y))\bigr).
$$

For the disk, an interior point already has zero projection distance. If $\norm z_2>1$, the triangle inequality gives $\norm{z-u}_2\geq\norm z_2-1$ for every feasible u. Equality holds at $u=z/\norm z_2$, so

$$
P_{\mathrm{disk}}(z)=\frac{z}{\max(1,\norm z_2)}.
$$

This also has a direct KKT check. Use Lagrangian $\frac12\norm{u-z}_2^2+\frac\mu2(\norm u_2^2-1)$. For an exterior z, choose $u=z/\norm z_2$ and $\mu=\norm z_2-1$. Then

$$
u-z+\mu u=0,\qquad \norm u_2^2=1,\qquad
\mu\geq0,\qquad \mu(\norm u_2^2-1)=0.
$$

Feasibility, stationarity, and complementarity identify the unique nearest point for this convex projection problem. They concern distance to z, not minimizing L in one operation.

On the whole plane, the penalized quadratic has gradient $((a+2\lambda)x-ac,(4+2\lambda)y)$. Its positive-definite Hessian gives the unique minimum

$$
\theta_J^*=\left(\frac{ac}{a+2\lambda},0\right),\qquad
J_{\min}=\frac{a\lambda c^2}{a+2\lambda}.
$$

For no hard constraint, errors around this changed target multiply by $1-\eta(a+2\lambda)$ and $1-\eta(4+2\lambda)$. Increasing the penalty increases curvature as well as changing the target; it need not rescue an excessive rate.

## Worked examples: box repair, radial repair, and a soft preference

At $(0,1)$ with $a=1,c=2$, the base loss is 4. The first box step is

$$
g_0=(-2,4),\qquad z_0=(2,-3),\qquad
\theta_1=(1,-1),\qquad L_1=5/2.
$$

Its correction and actual displacement are different:

$$
q_0=(1,-1)-(2,-3)=(-1,2),\qquad
d_0=(1,-1)-(0,1)=(1,-2).
$$

The next gradient is $(-1,-4)$, proposal $(2,3)$, and projected point $(1,1)$. Subsequent y values alternate between $-1$ and 1 at this rate, with loss $5/2$. The constrained minimizer is $(1,0)$ with loss $1/2$. Every iterate is feasible, yet this run does not approach that minimum. At the minimizer the raw gradient is $(-1,0)$, while projection makes the actual displacement zero.

With the same first proposal and the disk instead of the box,

$$
\theta_1=\frac{(2,-3)}{\sqrt{13}},\qquad
q_0=\left(\frac2{\sqrt{13}}-2,-\frac3{\sqrt{13}}+3\right),\qquad
d_0=\left(\frac2{\sqrt{13}},-\frac3{\sqrt{13}}-1\right).
$$

Its norm is 1, with projection multiplier $\sqrt{13}-1$. Its next base loss is $46/13-4/\sqrt{13}\approx2.42906$. Coordinate clamping to $(1,-1)$ would leave the disk.

For the penalty preset, use no hard constraint, $\lambda=1$, and $\eta=1/10$. At the same start, $J_0=5$ and $\nabla J=(-2,6)$, giving

$$
\theta_1=(1/5,2/5),\qquad
L_1=97/50,\qquad
J_1=107/50=2.14.
$$

Here $\theta_J^*=(2/3,0)$, $J_{\min}=4/3$, and $H_J=\operatorname{diag}(3,6)$. The stronger penalty $\lambda=3$ starts at $J_0=7$, uses gradient $(-2,10)$, and reaches $(1/5,0)$ with $J_1=87/50$. Its preferred x coordinate is $2/7$. These are changes to the optimization problem, not projections onto a hard region.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Projection acts on the proposal, not the gradient coordinates or the original requested start forever. The box clamp is not the disk rule. Projection minimizes Euclidean distance to its input; it does not solve the constrained loss problem in a single update.

The penalty contributes $2\lambda\theta$ to the gradient and $2\lambda I$ to the Hessian. Adding it only to a displayed loss would leave the wrong optimizer recurrence. Likewise, calling J the base loss would conceal the preference being traded against data fit.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="A finite penalty does not enforce a hard boundary">
For a=4, c=2, and λ=1, the whole-plane penalized minimum is (4/3,0). It lies outside the unit disk and the box. Projection enforces those regions; a finite L2 penalty changes the objective.
</Callout>

A feasible path can oscillate, and a constrained minimum can have nonzero raw gradient. At $(1,0)$ in this example, the negative gradient points beyond the allowed region and projection returns the same point. An unconstrained raw-gradient stopping rule would miss that constrained minimum.

## Numerical considerations

The stage and NumPy path use binary64 approximations. Disk normalization generally involves an irrational norm, so displayed coordinates use approximation signs. A computed boundary norm can differ slightly from 1 through rounding; the references check feasibility and KKT residuals with stated numerical tolerances.

Retain the proposed and projected values even when rounded labels appear identical. A small projection correction or small actual displacement can disappear at display precision. A large raw gradient at a boundary need not imply a large feasible displacement.

The high-precision reference supplies an independent numerical check, not symbolic exactness for every square root. Its positive curvature, nonnegative penalty, finite inputs, and bounded iteration count are validated. Comparing J between different penalty settings also changes the quantity being compared.

## Engineering and ML connections

Hard parameter limits can encode an admissible physical range or a norm budget. L2 regularization instead adds a cost for parameter size, changing the balance of the modeled objective. The coefficient and parameter units matter; the same numeric penalty after rescaling parameters describes a different preference.
