## Intuition: a shadow on a line

Imagine dropping a perpendicular from $\mathbf v$'s tip onto the line through the origin in direction $\mathbf u$. The point where it lands is the tip of the projection vector. The move from that point to $\mathbf v$'s tip is the residual. This splits one move into “along the line” and “away from the line.”

The direction vector determines a whole line, not only the ray in which its arrow points. Consequently a projection may have a negative coefficient. Replacing $\mathbf u$ by another nonzero multiple leaves the line and the vector projection unchanged. An oriented scalar component can change sign when the direction reverses.

## Definition

For a **nonzero** direction $\mathbf u$,

$$
\alpha=\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u},\qquad
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v=\alpha\mathbf u,\qquad
\mathbf r=\mathbf v-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v.
$$

The signed **scalar projection** is

$$
s=\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u}{\norm{\mathbf u}},\qquad
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v=s\widehat{\mathbf u}.
$$

These quantities have different meanings. $\alpha$ is the weight on the chosen vector $\mathbf u$. The scalar $s$ is signed distance along its unit direction. The vector projection has two components. The distance from $\mathbf v$'s tip to the entire line is the nonnegative number $\norm{\mathbf r}$.

## Mechanics: two checks on one decomposition

The inspector exposes the numerator, denominator, coefficient, projected vector, residual, and distance. A first check is reconstruction: projected vector plus residual equals $\mathbf v$. A second check is perpendicularity: $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf u=0$. Reconstruction alone is insufficient, because infinitely many arbitrary pairs add to $\mathbf v$; perpendicularity identifies the orthogonal decomposition.

The original $\mathbf v$ may be zero. If $\mathbf u$ is nonzero, its projection and residual are then both zero, and the distance is zero. The forbidden case is zero **direction**, not zero data. The stage shows all direction-dependent quantities as undefined in that case.

## Derivation: force the residual to be orthogonal

Any vector parallel to $\mathbf u$ can be written $\alpha\mathbf u$. Choose $\alpha$ by requiring the remainder to be orthogonal:

$$
0=(\mathbf v-\alpha\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf u
=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u-\alpha(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u).
$$

Because $\mathbf u\ne0$, its squared norm is positive, so division gives the stated coefficient. Substitution proves residual orthogonality exactly. The coefficient is unique, hence so are the parallel vector and residual.

Why does this yield the closest point on the line? For any other line point $t\mathbf u$,

$$
\norm{\mathbf v-t\mathbf u}^2
=\norm{\mathbf r+(\alpha-t)\mathbf u}^2
=\norm{\mathbf r}^2+(\alpha-t)^2\norm{\mathbf u}^2.
$$

The cross term vanishes by orthogonality. The second term is nonnegative and becomes zero exactly at $t=\alpha$. Thus the projection minimizes distance to the line, and its residual length is that minimum. This is a geometric derivation, without needing a calculus optimizer.

## Worked examples

For $\mathbf u=(2,1)$ and $\mathbf v=(1,3)$, the numerator and denominator are both $5$, so $\alpha=1$. Projection $(2,1)$ plus residual $(-1,2)$ equals $(1,3)$. The check is $(-1,2)\cdot(2,1)=-2+2=0$. The distance is $\sqrt5$, while the signed scalar projection is $5/\sqrt5=\sqrt5$; these happen to agree in this example but are different quantities.

## Common mistakes

Dividing by $\norm{\mathbf u}$ and then multiplying by $\mathbf u$ mixes scalar and vector projection formulas. The vector formula requires squared length in its denominator unless $\mathbf u$ is already unit length. Another mistake measures distance to a ray or finite segment while using a formula for an entire line; those constrained problems require checking endpoints or allowed parameters.

## Numerical considerations

The bounded stage uses exact fractions for the coefficient and residual, so its orthogonality check is an exact zero rather than a rounded display coincidence. Direct floating-point division by a squared norm can fail when that square overflows or underflows. Normalizing after rescaling protects the direction calculation. It does not guarantee that every later dot product or residual is representable: enormous data can overflow the signed component, and subtracting nearly equal components can lose relative accuracy in a tiny residual. A small residual may be meaningful only to the precision of the input measurements. The displayed distance is an exact radical where supported, followed by a clearly marked approximation.

## Engineering and ML connections

Projection separates velocity along a track from sideways motion. It also measures the part of data explained by one direction and the part left over. This prepares the geometric view of least squares in later linear algebra: choose an approximation in a span and study the orthogonal residual.
