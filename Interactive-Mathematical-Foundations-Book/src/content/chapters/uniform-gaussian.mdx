## Intuition: shape controls area

A uniform distribution assigns the same density across a finite support. Equal-width subintervals inside that support receive equal probabilities. A Gaussian concentrates density near its mean and decreases smoothly into both tails. Equal-width intervals at different locations generally have different probabilities.

Neither density gives positive probability to a single point. A shaded interval has area; a solitary density height describes concentration per unit of value. This distinction remains true at the highest point of the Gaussian curve.

## Definition

For $a<b$, the continuous uniform density is $f(x)=1/(b-a)$ inside $[a,b]$ and zero outside. Its CDF is zero below $a$, $(x-a)/(b-a)$ within the support, and one above $b$. Endpoint density choices do not affect any interval probability.

A Gaussian, or normal, distribution with finite mean $\mu$ and positive standard deviation $\sigma$ has density

$$
f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

Its mathematical support is the whole real line. The standard normal has $\mu=0$ and $\sigma=1$; write its CDF as $\Phi$. The density integrates to one over the whole line. A zero-width uniform support or nonpositive Gaussian $\sigma$ is invalid for these continuous formulas; a point-mass model would be a separate definition.

## Mechanics: separate model and viewport

Choose uniform or normal mode, change its parameters, and move the interval bounds. The highlighted area, CDF difference, inspector, formula, and integration trace all describe the same state. In uniform mode, only the part of the interval inside the support contributes area.

In normal mode, the drawn curve is sampled most densely over $\mu\pm4\sigma$, with extra points at interval and viewport bounds. The viewport also includes the uniform support for comparison. These finite samples make a readable approximation; they do not define the distribution's support or renormalize its CDF. The numerical integration uses a separate positive-tail cutoff at $8$ standard deviations.

## Derivation: normalize and standardize

Uniform normalization requires density times support width to equal one. Thus interval probability is the overlap width divided by $b-a$:

$$
P(\ell\le X\le u)
=\frac{\max(0,\min(u,b)-\max(\ell,a))}{b-a},\qquad \ell\le u.
$$

For the Gaussian, set $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma$. The substitution $dx=\sigma\,dz$ cancels the density's $1/\sigma$ factor, yielding the standard-normal density. Therefore

$$
P(\ell\le X\le u)
=\Phi\!\left(\frac{u-\mu}{\sigma}\right)
-\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ell-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
$$

The positive scale preserves endpoint order. Because these models are continuous, including or excluding finite endpoints gives the same probability.

## Worked examples

Uniform$(-2,2)$ has support width $4$ and density $1/4$. Its interval $[-1,1]$ has width $2$, so the area is exactly $1/2$. The interval $[-3,3]$ overlaps the full support and has probability $1$, rather than $6\times1/4$.

## Explore

In uniform mode, move a fixed-width interval entirely within the support. Predict the unchanged area, then move it across an edge and predict the decrease. Collapse the interval bounds to one point: area becomes zero while the density at that point can remain positive.

In normal mode, compare intervals one standard deviation wide near the mean and far into a tail. Change $\mu$ while shifting the interval by the same amount. Then scale $\sigma$ and the interval's distances from $\mu$ together. Standardized bounds explain why those coordinated changes preserve probability.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Uniform means equal density within the support, not over the entire real line. Extending a constant positive height forever would give infinite area. Gaussian does not mean every symmetric interval has the same probability: its location and width relative to $\mu$ and $\sigma$ matter.

For a normal density, substituting variance for $\sigma$ changes both height and width. A curve drawn within four standard deviations also does not capture exactly all mass: about $0.000063342484$ lies beyond the two drawing limits in total.

## Numerical considerations

The Gaussian model integrates the positive tail from $|z|$ to $8$ using composite Simpson's rule with 2048 subdivisions, then uses symmetry to obtain a CDF value. Beyond $8$ standard deviations it truncates the one-sided tail approximately; that omitted mass is less than $6.4\times10^{-16}$, not mathematically zero.

The absolute CDF error budget is $10^{-9}$ against independent `math.erfc` values. An interval subtracts two approximate CDFs, so its absolute budget is $2\times10^{-9}$. This is not a promise of relative accuracy for tiny intervals or extreme tails. The $\pm4\sigma$ drawn curve is not claimed to have exactly normalized area.

## Engineering and ML connections

Uniform models describe deliberate random choices over bounded ranges. Gaussian models describe a specified noise or uncertainty shape with location and scale. A bell-shaped drawing alone does not establish that observed data are Gaussian. The interval calculation is conditional on the chosen model, and density values must be interpreted with their measurement units.
