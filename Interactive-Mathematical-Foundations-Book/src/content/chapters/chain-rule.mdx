## Intuition: sensitivities multiply

In a pipeline $x\to u=g(x)\to y=f(u)$, a small change $\Delta x$ becomes $\Delta u\approx g'(x)\Delta x$, and that becomes $\Delta y\approx f'(u)\Delta u$. Combining,

$$
\Delta y\approx f'(u)\,g'(x)\,\Delta x .
$$

Each stage contributes its local amplification factor, and factors multiply — just as two gearboxes in a row multiply their ratios.

## Definition

If $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ is differentiable at $u=g(x)$, then $h=f\circ g$ is differentiable at $x$ and

$$
h'(x)=f'(g(x))\,g'(x),
$$

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\,\frac{du}{dx}.
$$

The outer derivative is evaluated at the **intermediate value** $u=g(x)$, not at $x$.

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

The three planes share one state. The inner plane shows $g$, its tangent at $x$ and the actual change $\Delta u$; the outer plane shows $f$, its tangent at $u$ and the actual change $\Delta y$; the composite plane shows $f(g(x))$ and its tangent with the chain-rule slope. When both stages are polynomials, the inspector also composes them exactly and differentiates the result — an independent route that must agree exactly. For $\sin$ and $e^u$, values are binary64 and marked $\approx$.

## Derivation

Write the outer function's local model at $u$ as

$$
f(u+k)=f(u)+f'(u)\,k+\varepsilon(k)\,k,
$$

where $\varepsilon(k)\to0$ as $k\to0$ (this is differentiability of $f$). Put $k=\Delta u=g(x+\Delta x)-g(x)$ and divide by $\Delta x$:

$$
\begin{aligned}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}&=\big(f'(u)+\varepsilon(\Delta u)\big)\\ &\quad\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}.\end{aligned}
$$

As $\Delta x\to0$, $\Delta u\to0$ (because $g$ is continuous), so $\varepsilon(\Delta u)\to0$ and $\Delta u/\Delta x\to g'(x)$. Hence $h'(x)=f'(u)g'(x)$. This form avoids dividing by $\Delta u$, which could be zero.

## Worked example

Take $g(x)=x^2/2-1$, $f(u)=u^3/4$, $x=2$:

* forward: $u=g(2)=1$, $y=f(1)=1/4$;
* local factors: $g'(2)=2$, $f'(1)=3/4$;
* backward product: $dy/dx=\tfrac34\cdot2=\tfrac32$.

Composed directly, $h(x)=\tfrac14(x^2/2-1)^3$ and $h'(x)=\tfrac34(x^2/2-1)^2\cdot x$, which is $\tfrac32$ at $x=2$. For $\Delta x=1/2$ the linearized change is $3/4$, while the actual change is $f(g(2.5))-f(1)=2.149\ldots$: the chain rule is exact for rates, approximate for finite steps.

## Toward computational graphs

The replay ends by multiplying **backward**: start with $dy/dy=1$, multiply by $f'(u)$, then by $g'(x)$. In a longer chain $x\to u_1\to u_2\to\cdots\to y$, the same backward product needs one multiplication per stage. When an input feeds several paths, the contributions of the paths add. Chapter III.10 turns this into an algorithm on a graph of elementary operations — reverse-mode automatic differentiation — the computation behind backpropagation. No neural network is needed to understand it.

## Explore

Swap the inner and outer roles: $\sin(2x-1)$ versus $2\sin x-1$ are different functions with different derivatives. Find an $x$ where the outer slope is zero but the inner slope is not. Increase $\Delta x$ and watch the actual $\Delta y$ leave the linear prediction.

## Common mistakes

Evaluating $f'$ at $x$ instead of $g(x)$. Forgetting the inner factor: $(\sin x^2)'=\cos(x^2)\cdot2x$, not $\cos(x^2)$. Treating $dy/du\cdot du/dx$ as cancelling fractions without the differentiability conditions that make it valid.

## Numerical considerations

Long chains multiply many factors. Factors with magnitude below $1$ shrink the product toward zero, factors above $1$ inflate it — the vanishing and exploding gradients of deep networks. In binary64 the product can underflow or overflow even when each factor is ordinary, which is why implementations often work with logarithms or normalised quantities (VII.7).

## Engineering and ML connections

Every layer of a neural network is a stage $u_{k+1}=g_k(u_k)$; the gradient of the loss with respect to an early parameter is a chain-rule product through all later layers. The same rule propagates measurement uncertainty through a sequence of formulas and units through a pipeline of conversions.

## Summary and checkpoints

For nested functions, local rates multiply: $h'(x)=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$, with the outer derivative evaluated at the intermediate value. The backward product of local factors is the seed of automatic differentiation. Next, III.7 lets a function depend on two inputs at once.
