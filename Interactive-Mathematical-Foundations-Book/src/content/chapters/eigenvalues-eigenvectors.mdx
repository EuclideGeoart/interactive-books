## Intuition: special directions simplify repeated action

Most vectors change both length and direction under a matrix. Some remain on the same line through the origin. Along such a direction, the full matrix acts like one scalar: stretch, reverse or collapse. Repeating the map multiplies that scalar repeatedly, which can explain growth and decay without multiplying the full matrix at every step.

Staying on the **same line** allows reversal; it does not require pointing the same way. A negative gain is therefore as meaningful as a positive one.

## Definition

For square $A$, an eigenvector is a **nonzero** vector $v$ such that

$$
Av=\lambda v.
$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is its eigenvalue. Positive $\lambda$ preserves its orientation, negative $\lambda$ reverses it, and $\lambda=0$ collapses the direction into the origin. The zero vector satisfies the equation for every scalar but is excluded: it identifies no direction.

All vectors associated with one eigenvalue, together with zero, form its eigenspace $\ker(A-\lambda I)$. A basis of that space counts independent eigen-directions; scaling an eigenvector does not create a new independent direction.

## Derivation: why the characteristic equation appears

Rearrange the definition as $(A-\lambda I)v=0$. A nonzero null vector exists exactly when $A-\lambda I$ is singular. Thus

$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=0.
$$

For $A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}$ this expands to

$$
(a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc
=\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc)=0.
$$

Write $t=\operatorname{tr}A$, $\delta=\det A$ and $D=t^2-4\delta$. When $D\ge0$ the roots are $(t\pm\sqrt D)/2$. Exact rational arithmetic decides the sign of $D$; the existing radical representation preserves simple exact roots. A numerical approximation is labeled $\approx$, and its eigenpair residual is checked separately.

## Worked examples

For $A=\operatorname{diag}(2,-1)$, the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2-\lambda-2=(\lambda-2)(\lambda+1)$. The x-axis stretches by $2$, while the y-axis reverses by $-1$. Starting with $(1,1)$, repeated applications give $(2,-1)$, $(4,1)$, $(8,-1)$ and $(16,1)$. The x-component grows; the y-component alternates.

## Repeated roots: scalar versus defective

The matrix $2I$ has eigenvalue $2$ repeated twice, and **every** nonzero input is an eigenvector. Any two independent directions can serve as an eigenbasis; the displayed axes are one convenient choice.

By contrast, $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ also has a repeated root, but $(A-I)v=(v_2,0)=0$ forces $v_2=0$. Only one independent direction exists. This is a defective matrix. Its shear behavior cannot be explained by two independent real eigenvectors; repeated root count is not eigenspace dimension.

## No real eigenvector

For the quarter-turn $Q=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation is $\lambda^2+1=0$. No real scalar satisfies it. Geometrically, every nonzero vector rotates through a right angle and leaves its original line. Over complex numbers the eigenvalues are $\pm i$. This explains the boundary of the real picture; complex eigenvectors and complex geometry are outside this chapter.

The zero-eigenvalue preset instead has a real nonzero null direction. Collapse is allowed as an eigenvalue behavior; it is the zero **input vector** that is excluded.

## Common mistakes

Eigenvectors need not be unit vectors, and eigenvalues need not be positive. A repeated eigenvalue does not guarantee two independent eigenvectors. A matrix with real entries may have no real eigenvector. The determinant condition finds candidate eigenvalues; finding independent null vectors still determines the eigenspaces.

## Numerical considerations

The exact discriminant class is independent of display precision. Numerical companions use binary64 and a residual bound of $256\epsilon\max(1,\|A\|_F)$ for unit directions, where $\epsilon$ is machine epsilon. This conservative rounding envelope is validated independently against Decimal roots and NumPy eigenpairs; it never classifies an exact zero.

The cancellation-prone quadratic root is recovered using $\lambda_1\lambda_2=\det A$. Near defective matrices can have highly sensitive eigendirections even with small residuals. If distinct numerical roots cannot be separated or the residual check fails, the lesson reports a numerical limitation instead of fabricating a direction.

## Engineering and ML connections

Eigen-directions describe repeated linear dynamics and stability, and symmetric eigenproblems underlie covariance analysis and PCA. General matrices can have complex or defective spectra, so a real eigenbasis is not universal. SVD will provide an orthogonal input/output decomposition for every real matrix, using distinct left and right directions.
