## Intuition: one knob at a time

A function $f(x,y)$ assigns a height to every point of the plane. Its **contour map** draws the curves $f=c$ at several heights, like an elevation map. Standing at a point, you can ask how the height changes if you walk east (change $x$ only) or north (change $y$ only). Each question fixes one input and leaves an ordinary function of one variable, whose derivative you already know.

## Definition

The **partial derivatives** of $f$ at $(a,b)$ are

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h},
$$

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}{h}.
$$

Equivalently, $\partial f/\partial x$ is the derivative of the **slice** $t\mapsto f(t,b)$ at $t=a$. Other common notations are $f_x$, $\partial_x f$ and $D_1f$.

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

The surfaces are polynomials with rational coefficients, so the height, both slices and both partial derivatives are exact. The contour lines are a drawing aid computed numerically on a grid; no value is read from them. Each slice plane shows the one-variable polynomial obtained by fixing the other input, and the inspector adds an exact secant along each axis with $h=0.25$ as evidence.

## Derivation: differentiate the slice

For $f(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ at $(1,\tfrac12)$, fix $y=\tfrac12$:

$$
f(t,\tfrac12)=t^2-\tfrac14 ,
$$

whose derivative $2t$ is $2$ at $t=1$. Fix $x=1$ instead:

$$
f(1,t)=1-t^2 ,
$$

whose derivative $-2t$ is $-1$ at $t=\tfrac12$. In practice we write this as a rule: differentiate with respect to one variable, treating the others as constants. So $\partial f/\partial x=2x$ and $\partial f/\partial y=-2y$.

## Worked example

Take $f(x,y)=xy$ at $(1,\tfrac12)$. The $x$-slice is $f(t,\tfrac12)=\tfrac12t$, a line of slope $\tfrac12$; the $y$-slice is $f(1,t)=t$, slope $1$. Hence $\partial f/\partial x=y=\tfrac12$ and $\partial f/\partial y=x=1$. Because $f$ is linear in each variable separately, every secant along an axis equals the partial derivative exactly.

## What partial derivatives do not say

Two partial derivatives describe change along two directions only. A function can have both partial derivatives at a point and still behave badly along a diagonal; and zero partial derivatives do not identify a minimum (the saddle at the origin). III.8 combines the partials into the gradient, which describes change in every direction for differentiable functions, and III.11 uses second derivatives to classify flat points.

## Explore

On **Tilted plane** the partials are the same everywhere: a plane has constant slopes. On **Valley**, compare how much faster the height changes along $y$ than along $x$ — the contours are squeezed in the $y$ direction. Move the point along a contour line and notice that the height row stays fixed while the partials change.

## Common mistakes

Holding the wrong variable fixed, or forgetting to treat it as a constant ($\partial(x^2y)/\partial x=2xy$, not $2x$). Reading a partial derivative as the total change when both inputs move. Assuming zero partial derivatives mean a minimum.

## Numerical considerations

Grid-based partial derivatives (as in `numpy.gradient`) are finite differences with spacing errors; at boundaries they become one-sided and less accurate. Contour plots depend on the grid resolution and chosen levels and can miss narrow features. Exact slices avoid both issues for polynomials.

## Engineering and ML connections

A model's loss depends on many parameters at once. Each partial derivative measures sensitivity to one parameter while all others are fixed; together they form the gradient used by optimisation (VI.4). Partial derivatives also describe sensitivities of physical models — temperature to position, cost to each resource.
