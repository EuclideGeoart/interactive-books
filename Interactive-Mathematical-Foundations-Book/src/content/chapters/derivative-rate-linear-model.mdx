## Intuition: rate and prediction

If $s(t)$ is a position, the secant slope $\frac{s(t+\Delta t)-s(t)}{\Delta t}$ is the **average velocity** over the interval, and the derivative $s'(t)$ is the **instantaneous velocity**. Knowing where you are and how fast you are going, you can predict where you will be shortly:

$$
s(t+\Delta t)\approx s(t)+s'(t)\,\Delta t .
$$

The same reading applies to any quantity: a cost per extra unit, a temperature change per second, a loss change per parameter step.

## Definition

The **local linear model** (linearization, tangent-line approximation) of a differentiable $f$ at $x_0$ is

$$
L(\Delta x)=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)\,\Delta x .
$$

Its **error** is $e(\Delta x)=f(x_0+\Delta x)-L(\Delta x)$. Differentiability at $x_0$ means exactly that the error is small compared with the displacement:

$$
\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{e(\Delta x)}{\Delta x}=0 .
$$

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

For the cubic $f(x)=x^3/6-x$ every quantity is exact: $f(x_0)$, the derivative, the model value, the true value and the error. For $\sin x$ and $e^x$ the values are binary64 and marked $\approx$. The error plane draws $e(\Delta x)$ from the same model, together with the dashed parabola $\tfrac12f''(x_0)\Delta x^2$ that describes it near $0$. The table halves $\Delta x$ and lists $e$, $e/\Delta x$ and $e/\Delta x^2$.

## Derivation: why the error is second order

For a polynomial, expanding around $x_0$ gives exactly

$$
\begin{aligned}f(x_0+\Delta x)={}&f(x_0)+f'(x_0)\Delta x\\ &+\tfrac12f''(x_0)\Delta x^2+\cdots\end{aligned}
$$

so the error starts with $\tfrac12f''(x_0)\Delta x^2$. Dividing by $\Delta x$ leaves $\tfrac12f''(x_0)\Delta x+\cdots$, which tends to $0$. Halving $\Delta x$ therefore roughly quarters the error when $f''(x_0)\ne0$. When $f''(x_0)=0$ (an inflection) the error is even smaller, of order $\Delta x^3$. Far from $x_0$ the higher terms dominate and nothing keeps the error small.

## Worked example

Take $f(x)=x^3/6-x$, $x_0=1$, $\Delta x=1$. Then $f(1)=-5/6$ and $f'(1)=-1/2$, so the model predicts

$$
L(1)=-\tfrac56-\tfrac12=-\tfrac43 .
$$

The true value is $f(2)=8/6-2=-2/3$, so $e(1)=-2/3+4/3=2/3$. With $\Delta x=1/2$, $e=7/48$; with $\Delta x=1/4$, $e=13/384$. The ratio $e/\Delta x^2$ approaches $\tfrac12f''(1)=\tfrac12$.

## When there is no linear model

At a corner, such as $|x|$ at $0$, no single slope works on both sides. Any line through the origin misses one side by an amount proportional to $|\Delta x|$, so $e/\Delta x$ cannot tend to $0$. The explorer reports the one-sided slopes and refuses to draw a model.

## Explore

Put $x_0$ at an inflection ($x_0=0$ for the cubic) and compare the error curve with the flat dashed parabola. On $e^x$ at $0$, the model $1+\Delta x$ always underestimates. Make $\Delta x$ negative: the model also predicts the past.

## Common mistakes

Evaluating the derivative at $x_0+\Delta x$ instead of $x_0$ mixes two different models. An average rate over a long interval is not the instantaneous rate. "Small $\Delta x$" is not a fixed number: it is small relative to how quickly the slope changes ($f''$).

## Numerical considerations

The model is often used in binary64 to predict tiny changes, and the error of the prediction can be much smaller than the rounding error of computing $f(x_0+\Delta x)-f(x_0)$ directly. That is why linearized error propagation (VIII.3) is a practical tool, and why finite differences (III.14) need care.

## Engineering and ML connections

Gradient descent (VI.4) trusts the local linear model to choose a downhill step; a too-large learning rate leaves the region where the model is accurate, exactly like **Far away**. Sensitivity analysis, uncertainty propagation and Newton's method all start from $f(x_0)+f'(x_0)\Delta x$.
