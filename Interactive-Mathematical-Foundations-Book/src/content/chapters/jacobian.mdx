## Intuition: zoom until it is linear

A differentiable function of one variable looks like a line when you zoom in. A differentiable map of the plane looks like a **linear transformation** when you zoom in: small grids become grids of parallelograms. That linear transformation is the Jacobian matrix. Its columns say where the two input step directions go; its determinant says how small areas are scaled.

## Definition

For $F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ with components $F_1,\dots,F_m$, the **Jacobian** at $p$ is the $m\times n$ matrix

$$
J_F(p)=\left[\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}(p)\right],
$$

with one row per output and one column per input. Row $i$ is the gradient of $F_i$; column $j$ is the response of all outputs to the $j$-th input. Differentiability means

$$
F(p+\boldsymbol\delta)=F(p)+J\boldsymbol\delta+o(\|\boldsymbol\delta\|).
$$

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

The maps are polynomial, so $J$, $F(p)$, the prediction $F(p)+J\boldsymbol\delta$ and the independent evaluation $F(p+\boldsymbol\delta)$ are exact; the squared error is an exact rational. The output plane is a local window centred near $F(p)$. Its curved shape samples the true image of the square's boundary; the parallelogram and the two column arrows are drawn from $J$. The **Lift** preset maps $\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^3$: its $3\times2$ Jacobian multiplies 2-vectors and returns 3-vectors, so the plane shows only two of the three outputs and says so.

## Derivation: from partial derivatives to a matrix

Each component is a scalar function, so by III.8, $F_i(p+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx F_i(p)+\nabla F_i\cdot\boldsymbol\delta$. Stacking the $m$ rows gives the matrix–vector product $J\boldsymbol\delta$. Multiplying by $\boldsymbol\delta=\delta_1\mathbf e_1+\delta_2\mathbf e_2$ combines the columns: $J\boldsymbol\delta=\delta_1J\mathbf e_1+\delta_2J\mathbf e_2$. Shapes must agree: an $m\times n$ matrix multiplies only an $n$-vector.

## Worked example

For $F=(x^2-y^2,\,2xy)$ at $p=(1,\tfrac12)$:

$$
J=\begin{bmatrix}2x&-2y\\2y&2x\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{bmatrix},
$$

so $\det J=5$.

$F(p)=(\tfrac34,1)$. With $\boldsymbol\delta=(\tfrac14,\tfrac14)$, $J\boldsymbol\delta=(\tfrac14,\tfrac34)$, so the prediction is $(1,\tfrac74)$. The independent evaluation is $F(\tfrac54,\tfrac34)=(1,\tfrac{15}8)$. The error $(0,\tfrac18)$ is second order: halving $\boldsymbol\delta$ quarters it.

## Singular and rectangular Jacobians

At the origin the complex square has $J=0$: every first-order change vanishes and the square shrinks by a factor of order $\|\boldsymbol\delta\|$ more than linear scaling predicts. A square Jacobian with $\det J=0$ is not invertible, so the map cannot be locally inverted there. A rectangular Jacobian has no determinant; for $\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^3$ the image of a small square is a small parallelogram tilted in space. Multiplying a $2\times2$ Jacobian by a 3-vector is rejected as a shape mismatch.

## Explore

On **Linear map**, the prediction is exact for every $\boldsymbol\delta$: the Jacobian of a linear map is the map. On **Bend**, notice that the second row never changes: $F_2=y$ has gradient $(0,1)$. Move $\boldsymbol\delta$ along $\mathbf e_1$ and watch the orange arrow align with the first column.

## Common mistakes

Transposing the Jacobian (rows are outputs, columns are inputs). Multiplying in the wrong order: $J\boldsymbol\delta$, not $\boldsymbol\delta J$. Expecting a determinant from a non-square Jacobian. Trusting the linear prediction far from $p$.

## Numerical considerations

Near-singular Jacobians make local inversion (Newton's method for systems) sensitive: small output errors become large input errors, as with ill-conditioned matrices in Part II. Finite-difference Jacobians cost one extra evaluation per input column and inherit the step-size trade-off of III.14.

## Engineering and ML connections

The Jacobian of each layer maps input perturbations to output perturbations; backpropagation multiplies transposed Jacobians from the loss backward (III.10). Robotics uses manipulator Jacobians to relate joint and hand velocities; change-of-variables formulas in probability use $|\det J|$.

## Summary and checkpoints

The Jacobian is the matrix of all first partial derivatives, $m\times n$ for $F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$. It is the local linear transformation of the map: $F(p+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx F(p)+J\boldsymbol\delta$, with columns as input responses and rows as output gradients.
