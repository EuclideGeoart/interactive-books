## Intuition: coordinates depend on a measuring frame

The standard components $(3,0)$ describe a point relative to one horizontal and one vertical unit direction. A different pair of directions can describe the same target with different weights. Coordinates are instructions for reconstructing a vector from a chosen basis; they are not an intrinsic pair attached to the target independently of the frame.

A useful basis supplies enough directions to reach every target and avoids redundant directions that would give multiple descriptions. The stage holds $\mathbf p$ fixed when a basis tip moves, so the changing coordinates reveal a change of description rather than a motion of the target.

## Definition

Two vectors are **linearly independent** when

$$
a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v=\mathbf0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a=b=0.
$$

A **basis of $\mathbb R^2$** is an ordered pair of independent vectors that spans the plane. In two dimensions, two independent vectors already span the plane. For a chosen ordered basis $(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)$, the coordinates of $\mathbf p$ are the unique pair $(a,b)$ satisfying

$$
\mathbf p=a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v.
$$

Order matters: swapping the basis vectors swaps their coordinate roles. Orthogonality and unit lengths are convenient but are not required. A slanted, unequal-length independent pair is still a basis.

## Mechanics: reconstruction and singularity

The blue and green basis arrows show the two directions. The head-to-tail weighted arrows reconstruct the target. Grid lines correspond to fixed basis coefficients, so moving the basis changes the coordinate grid. The inspector keeps the standard target components separate from the solved basis coordinates.

The stage's independence test is the exact two-dimensional quantity

$$
\Delta=u_xv_y-u_yv_x.
$$

This is the determinant of the two basis columns. Here it is a practical independence test; its full area, orientation, and higher-dimensional interpretation belongs to II.12. If $\Delta=0$, the pair is singular and cannot be a basis of the plane. The stage then tests whether the target lies in the surviving span, and distinguishes **no representation** from **infinitely many representations**. It never supplies a fabricated unique coordinate pair.

## Derivation: an independent pair has unique weights

The component equations are

$$
u_xa+v_xb=p_x,\qquad u_ya+v_yb=p_y.
$$

Multiply the first by $v_y$, the second by $v_x$, and subtract. The $b$ terms cancel, leaving $\Delta a=p_xv_y-p_yv_x$. Eliminate $a$ similarly. For $\Delta\ne0$,

$$
a=\frac{p_xv_y-p_yv_x}{\Delta},\qquad
b=\frac{u_xp_y-u_yp_x}{\Delta}.
$$

These formulas give a representation for every target. To prove uniqueness, suppose another pair $(a',b')$ gives the same target. Subtract the two representations: $(a-a')\mathbf u+(b-b')\mathbf v=\mathbf0$. Independence forces both differences to vanish.

If the two directions are collinear, a nonzero combination can cancel them. For instance, $\mathbf v=2\mathbf u$ gives $2\mathbf u-\mathbf v=\mathbf0$, proving dependence. Any reachable target then has infinitely many representations because adding a multiple of this cancelling pair changes the coefficients without changing the vector. A zero basis vector immediately creates such redundancy.

## Worked examples

For $\mathbf u=(2,1)$, $\mathbf v=(1,2)$, and $\mathbf p=(3,0)$, the determinant is $3$. The formulas yield $a=2$ and $b=-1$, checked by $2(2,1)-(1,2)=(3,0)$. The target's standard components $(3,0)$ and its basis coordinates $(2,-1)$ describe the same vector in different frames.

## Common mistakes

Two nonzero vectors can still be dependent. Two perpendicular nonzero vectors are independent, but perpendicularity is sufficient rather than necessary. A reachable target does not prove the pair spans the plane, and a small reconstruction residual alone does not prove the computed coordinates are accurate. Also distinguish basis change from moving the target: changing the measuring frame changes the numbers describing the same vector.

## Numerical considerations

Exact independence asks whether $\Delta$ is zero. Numerical reliability asks how sensitive the coordinates are to errors in the vectors and target. The stage reports the relative directional separation $|\sin\theta|$, which removes the vectors' overall scale. Below $10^{-4}$ it warns about sensitivity; this warning never changes exact independence. The threshold is a teaching cue for this bounded scene, not a general solver's rank decision.

Near dependence, large weighted moves nearly cancel. Rounding can then alter the final target substantially, even when each large move changes only slightly. NumPy's condition estimate previews this issue; practical rank and solver tolerances require the scale and uncertainty of actual data. Exact arithmetic on entered decimals certifies the intended reconstruction but cannot repair inaccurate measurements. The stage rejects invalid numeric input and shows fractions exactly, while rounded decimal companions use $\approx$.

## Engineering and ML connections

A basis is a coordinate system for signals, motions, or features. Redundant directions can create multiple parameter settings with the same output; changing a basis changes the weights without necessarily changing the represented object. The column convention also prepares a transformation view of matrices: specify where the standard basis directions go, and every other vector follows by the same weighted-combination rule.
