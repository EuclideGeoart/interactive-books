## Intuition: how much does each refinement buy?

Every rule replaces $f$ on each panel by something simpler — a constant for rectangle rules, a straight line for the trapezoid rule. Halving the panel width $h$ makes the replacement better. How much better depends on how closely the simple shape follows the curve, which depends on the rule **and** on how smooth $f$ is. Plotting $\log(\text{error})$ against the refinement level turns a rate $h^p$ into a straight line with slope $p$.

## Definition

With $n$ panels of width $h=(b-a)/n$ and points $x_i=a+ih$:

$$
L_n=h\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(x_i),
$$

$$
M_n=h\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f\Big(\frac{x_i+x_{i+1}}2\Big),
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}T_n=h\Big(&\tfrac12f(x_0)+f(x_1)+\cdots\\&+f(x_{n-1})+\tfrac12f(x_n)\Big),\end{aligned}
$$

and $R_n$ uses right endpoints. A rule has **order** $p$ for a class of functions if its error behaves like $Ch^p$. The **observed order** from two refinements is $p\approx\log_2(E_{n}/E_{2n})$.

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

For $x^2$, $x^3-x$ and $|x-1/3|$ every approximation and every error is an exact rational, computed for $n=1,2,4,\dots,1024$. For $\sin x$ and $\sqrt x$ the values are binary64, with references $1-\cos3$ and $2/3$ from their antiderivatives. Panels are drawn while they are wider than a pixel. The convergence plot and table come from the same computed errors.

## Derivation: why the trapezoid rule has order 2

On one panel $[x_i,x_i+h]$, the trapezoid replaces $f$ by its linear interpolant. The interpolation error is $\tfrac12f''(\xi)(t-x_i)(t-x_i-h)$, and integrating over the panel gives $-\tfrac{h^3}{12}f''(\xi_i)$. Summing $n=(b-a)/h$ panels,

$$
T_n-\int_a^b f=-\frac{(b-a)\,h^2}{12}f''(\xi)
$$

for some $\xi$, provided $f''$ is continuous. So halving $h$ quarters the error. A rectangle rule replaces $f$ by a constant and loses a factor $h$: order $1$. The midpoint rule is order $2$ with half the trapezoid's constant and the opposite sign.

## Worked example

$\int_0^1x^2\,dx=1/3$. With $n$ trapezoids the error is exactly $1/(6n^2)$: $n=1$ gives $1/2$ (error $1/6$), $n=2$ gives $3/8$ (error $1/24$), $n=4$ gives $11/32$ (error $1/96$). The midpoint error is exactly $-1/(12n^2)$, and the left-rule error is $-1/(2n)+1/(6n^2)$, which approaches order $1$.

## When the rate changes

* **Singular slope.** $\sqrt x$ has $f'\to\infty$ at $0$; the $h^2$ bound does not apply and the trapezoid error behaves like $h^{1.5}$.
* **A corner off the grid.** $|x-1/3|$ has a corner that never falls on a grid point $k/2^m$; the first refinements behave irregularly before settling.
* **Signed integrands.** For $x^3-x$ on $[-1,2]$, positive and negative parts partly cancel in the exact value $9/4$; the rules must resolve both.
* **No universal promise.** Highly oscillatory or discontinuous integrands can make any fixed rule converge slowly or appear to converge to the wrong value for small $n$.

## Common mistakes

Assuming more panels always gives the promised rate regardless of smoothness. Reading the error from a single $n$ instead of its behaviour under refinement. Confusing the number of panels with the number of function evaluations (the trapezoid rule uses $n+1$ points).

## Numerical considerations

For smooth integrands, higher-order rules (Simpson's rule, Gaussian quadrature) reach small errors with far fewer evaluations; adaptive methods refine only where the integrand needs it. At very large $n$ rounding in the sum eventually matters. Reliable software reports an error estimate, usually from comparing two refinements, exactly as the observed-order column does.

## Engineering and ML connections

Numerical integration computes expectations, normalising constants, areas under curves and solutions of differential equations (one step of many ODE methods is a quadrature rule). Monte Carlo integration (Part IV) trades the deterministic rates here for a rate of $n^{-1/2}$ that does not depend on dimension.

## Summary and checkpoints

Rectangle rules converge with order 1, trapezoid and midpoint rules with order 2 — for smooth integrands. Singularities and corners lower the observed order; signed integrands require resolving cancellation. Measure convergence by refinement rather than trusting a single approximation.
