## Intuition: binary trials, numerical totals

A trial records success as $1$ and failure as $0$. “Success” is simply the event being counted; it need not mean a desirable result. One trial is a Bernoulli variable. Adding several trials produces a success count.

For two fair independent trials, the sequences are $00,01,10,11$. Their counts are $0,1,1,2$. The same grouping principle from random variables yields three count values, with more mass in the middle because two sequences feed it.

## Definition

A **Bernoulli** variable $B$ with parameter $p\in[0,1]$ has $P(B=1)=p$ and $P(B=0)=1-p$. If $B_1,\ldots,B_n$ are independent Bernoulli variables with the same $p$, then

$$
X=\sum_{i=1}^n B_i\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p),
\qquad P(X=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\quad 0\le k\le n.
$$

Outside that integer support, the mass is zero. The parameter $n$ is a nonnegative integer; $n=0$ is valid. For $n=1$, the binomial model is Bernoulli. Independence and a common success probability are assumptions, not consequences of using binary labels.

## Mechanics: parameters and selected events

The stage accepts integer $n$ from $0$ through $30$, success percentage from $0$ through $100$ with two decimal places, and selected integer $k$ from $-1$ through $31$. These are teaching controls; divide a percentage by $100$ to obtain the probability parameter. The selected $k$ identifies one event without changing the distribution.

Follow the trace from selecting $k$ success positions, through the probability of one such sequence, to their product. Compare the selected PMF mass with its CDF jump. Selecting $k=-1$ or $k>n$ yields mass zero; it must not be silently clamped to a possible count. Invalid parameters, such as negative $n$ or $p>1$, instead fail to define this model.

## Derivation: multiply first, then count

Independence makes any particular length-$n$ sequence with $k$ successes have mass $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$. A common $p$ makes this mass the same for every sequence with that count. There are $\binom nk$ choices of success positions, giving the PMF above.

The binomial theorem checks its total:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}
=(p+(1-p))^n=1.
$$

With zero trials, the one empty sequence has success count $0$ and mass $1$. A zero exponent here denotes an empty product of value $1$, including boundary factors written $0^0$ in this combinatorial formula. This convention concerns empty products; it is not a universal statement about indeterminate limits.

## Worked examples

For $n=2$, $p=1/2$, all four sequences have mass $1/4$. Grouping gives $P(X=0)=1/4$, $P(X=1)=1/2$, $P(X=2)=1/4$. A single count label does not mean a single source sequence.

For $n=3$, $p=1/4$, exactly one success can occupy three positions. Each corresponding sequence has mass $(1/4)(3/4)^2=9/64$, so

$$
P(X=1)=3\times\frac9{64}=\frac{27}{64}.
$$

At $p=0$, all mass lies at $k=0$. At $p=1$, all mass lies at $k=n$. For $n=0$, these both name the same count. No factorial of a negative integer is needed for an impossible selected event.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Three possible counts for two trials are not equally likely. Counting labels would yield three masses $1/3$ and discard sequence multiplicity. Also, $p$ is a probability, not the percentage number: $25\%$ means $p=1/4$, not $p=25$.

Equal trial marginals alone are insufficient. If the second trial always copies the first fair trial, the count is $0$ or $2$, each with mass $1/2$, and never $1$. Independent trials with differing success probabilities also need a different count model; one common $p$ cannot encode them.

## Numerical considerations

Finite decimal percentages can be represented as exact rational inputs. Exact references avoid rounded factorial ratios by using integer combinations. The stage's bounded $n\le30$ keeps its examples small, but floating-point masses remain approximations unless an exact form is supplied.

Boundary values deserve deliberate checks: $n=0$, $p=0$, $p=1$, and impossible $k$ are valid cases with exact answers. Do not use a numerical epsilon to declare a small positive probability zero. General large-$n$ algorithms would require additional numerical care beyond this bounded lesson.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="An impossible count is not an invalid parameter">
A valid Binomial(2, 1/2) model assigns probability zero to count 3. A negative trial count or success probability above one is invalid input and should be reported as such.
</Callout>

## Engineering and ML connections

Binary indicators count errors, accepted requests, or successful tests. A binomial model is appropriate when independence and constant success probability are justified by the experiment. Reused inputs, common failures, or changing difficulty can violate those assumptions; a convenient formula does not repair the model.
