## Intuition: zooming in on a curve

A secant is the straight line through two points of a graph, $P=(x,f(x))$ and $Q=(x+h,f(x+h))$. Its slope is an ordinary average rate of change: rise over run. When $h$ is small, $Q$ is close to $P$ and the secant hugs the graph near $P$. For a smooth function the secant slopes approach one number. That number is the slope of the graph **at** $P$.

The important word is *approach*. The secant formula is never evaluated with $h=0$; the tangent slope is what the secant slopes tend to as $h$ shrinks through nonzero values.

## Definition

The **difference quotient** of $f$ at $x$ with step $h\ne0$ is

$$
m(h)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.
$$

The function $f$ is **differentiable** at $x$ when $f(x)$ exists and the two-sided limit

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
$$

exists as a finite real number. The **tangent line** at $P$ is then $y=f(x)+f'(x)(t-x)$. The one-sided limits $h\to0^-$ and $h\to0^+$ are the left and right slopes; the derivative exists exactly when both exist, are finite and agree.

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

Each secant slope is an actual quotient of two function values with a nonzero step. For the cubic $f(x)=x^3/6-x$ and decimal inputs, the values are exact rationals, so the inspector shows $=$. For $\sin x$ they are binary64 approximations, marked $\approx$. The tangent slope row is **not** read from the table: it comes from the function's analytic description (for a polynomial, the exact derivative; for a corner, the two exact one-sided slopes). The table is evidence that agrees with it, not a proof.

Moving $P$ changes $x$ and keeps $h$; moving $Q$ changes $h$. Arrow keys move the focused point by $0.25$, and the numeric fields accept two decimal places.

## Derivation: why the secant slope approaches the derivative

For a polynomial the quotient simplifies before any limit is taken. With $f(x)=x^2$,

$$
\begin{aligned}\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}&=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}\\ &=2x+h\qquad(h\ne0).\end{aligned}
$$

The cancellation of $h$ is legitimate only because $h\ne0$. The simplified expression $2x+h$ is continuous in $h$, so its limit as $h\to0$ is $2x$. In general, for a polynomial $p$, Taylor expansion gives

$$
m(h)=p'(x)+\tfrac12p''(x)\,h+\cdots,
$$

a polynomial in $h$ (the omitted terms carry $h^2$, $h^3$, …) whose constant term is $p'(x)$. The explorer prints this exact polynomial for the cubic: its constant term is the tangent slope and the remaining terms measure how far a finite secant is from the tangent.

## Worked example

Take $f(x)=x^3/6-x$ at $x=1$. Then $f(1)=-5/6$, and for every $h\ne0$ the exact difference quotient is

$$
m(h)=-\tfrac12+\tfrac12h+\tfrac16h^2
$$

With $h=1$ the secant slope is $1/6$; with $h=1/2$ it is $-5/24$; with $h=1/4$ it is $-35/96$. These approach $f'(1)=1^2/2-1=-1/2$. The replay below steps through the halving sequence and ends with the separately derived limit.

## When no derivative exists

* **Corner.** For $|x|$ at $0$, $m(h)=|h|/h$ equals $1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$. Both one-sided slopes exist, but they differ, so $f'(0)$ does not exist.
* **Vertical tangent.** For $\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $0$, $m(h)=h^{-2/3}\to+\infty$. The graph is smooth to the eye, but the slope is not a real number.
* **Endpoint.** $\sqrt x$ is defined only for $x\ge0$. At $0$ only $h>0$ is possible, and the right quotient $1/\sqrt h$ is unbounded.
* **Jump.** A function that is not continuous at $x$ cannot be differentiable there: from the broken side, $f(x+h)-f(x)$ does not shrink while $h$ does.

Every one of these is reported by the stage as an undefined derivative with its reason; none is replaced by a nearby number.

## Common mistakes

Substituting $h=0$ into the quotient produces $0/0$, which has no value. Cancelling $h$ is valid only while $h\ne0$. A derivative needs agreement from **both** sides; averaging two different one-sided slopes does not create one. A graph without a visible break can still fail to be differentiable (a vertical tangent), and a numerical table that looks like it settles is not a proof that a limit exists.

<Callout kind="misconception">A small secant slope table is evidence. The derivative is decided by the limit — for a polynomial, by the exact algebra above.</Callout>

## Numerical considerations

In exact rational arithmetic, smaller $h$ always brings the secant closer to the tangent of a polynomial. In binary64 it does not: $f(x+h)-f(x)$ subtracts two nearly equal numbers, and the rounding error is divided by a tiny $h$. Chapter III.14 measures this trade-off between truncation and rounding error. Here the halving table stops at $h/64$, far above that regime.

## Engineering and ML connections

Every gradient used to train a model is a collection of derivatives. Automatic differentiation computes them from the operations of a program (III.10) rather than from secants. Finite-difference quotients remain the standard independent check of a gradient implementation, provided the step size and its error are understood.

## Summary and checkpoints

A secant slope is a quotient with $h\ne0$. The derivative is the limit of these slopes as $h\to0$ from both sides, when that limit exists and is finite. Corners, vertical tangents, endpoints and jumps are places where it does not. Next, III.4 reads the derivative as a rate of change and uses the tangent line as a local linear model.
