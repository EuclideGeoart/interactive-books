## Intuition: mass lives on outcomes

Events do not own separate copies of probability. They select from a single space whose weights sum to one. An outcome that belongs to both events still has one weight. This is why overlapping regions cannot simply be added when the question is “A or B.”

The geometry of overlap is set membership. Probability measures its mass. Two regions with the same number of labels can have different probabilities when their weights differ.

## Definition

For events $A,B\subseteq\Omega$, the **union** $A\cup B$ contains outcomes in at least one event; the **intersection** $A\cap B$ contains outcomes in both. The **complement** $A^c=\Omega\setminus A$ contains outcomes outside $A$ but inside the chosen sample space. Events are **disjoint** when $A\cap B=\varnothing$.

For every event, $P(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}p_\omega$, using the same nonnegative, normalized weights. Therefore $0\le P(A)\le1$, $P(\varnothing)=0$, and $P(\Omega)=1$.

## Derivation: inclusion-exclusion

Separate the union into three disjoint pieces: $A\setminus B$, $A\cap B$, and $B\setminus A$. Adding $P(A)+P(B)$ includes the middle piece twice. Subtract it once:

$$
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B).
$$

The sample space also splits into the disjoint pieces $A$ and $A^c$, so

$$
P(A^c)=1-P(A).
$$

These identities require neither equal outcome weights nor independence. For disjoint events the intersection is empty, and the subtraction term is zero. For $A=B$, the union is simply $A$, not twice its mass.

## Worked examples

On the fair die, $P(A)=P(B)=1/2$. The intersection $\{4,6\}$ has mass $1/3$. The union $\{2,4,5,6\}$ has mass $2/3$, agreeing with $1/2+1/2-1/3$. The complement $A^c=\{1,3,5\}$ has mass $1/2$.

<Callout kind="scope" title="Zero overlap mass does not always mean empty overlap">
In a finite model with strictly positive weights, zero intersection mass means disjoint sets. If zero weights are allowed, two events can share a zero-mass outcome. Set disjointness is about membership; zero joint probability is about mass.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

“Or” in union is inclusive: an outcome in both counts. Exclusive OR would omit the intersection and describe a different event. A complement is relative to the declared space; the complement of even die faces does not include the integer $7$.

Equal probabilities do not imply equal sets. The even faces and the top three faces each have mass $1/2$ but differ. Disjointness also does not mean independence: odd and even are mutually exclusive and each has positive mass, so knowing one happened rules out the other.

## Numerical considerations

Rational summation preserves exact union and complement identities for these finite examples. Floating-point code may accumulate tiny summation errors. The NumPy reference checks its small arrays against exact fractions with absolute tolerance $10^{-12}$ and zero relative tolerance; it does not silently clip a negative weight or renormalize an invalid vector.

Boolean set membership itself is exact. Keep that separate from approximate mass arithmetic. A tiny rounded joint mass should not decide whether two sets are disjoint, and a displayed decimal such as $0.3333$ is not the exact fraction $1/3$.

## Engineering and ML connections

Boolean masks select rows, category groups, and evaluation subsets. A union of error categories requires overlap correction or an OR mask; adding category counts can double-count the same examples. Weighted data requires summing weights over the mask. The same structure appears when grouping predicted categorical probabilities.
