With a fair die, take $A=\{2,4,6\}$ and $B=\{4,5,6\}$. Given that B happened, the face must be 4, 5, or 6. Predict the chance of an even face before reading the equation.

## Intuition: information changes the reference total

Before observing B, all six die faces are relevant. After learning B, only three can explain the information. Of those, two are even. The original chance of evenness is $1/2$, the original shared mass is $1/3$, and the conditional chance is $2/3$. These are three different questions about the same experiment.

Restriction keeps only outcomes compatible with the evidence. Normalization rescales their total to one. It preserves relative weights among the survivors rather than inventing a new equal-weight model.

## Definition

For events $A,B$ with $P(B)>0$, **conditional probability** is

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.
$$

For an individual outcome in a finite space, the conditional mass is $p_\omega/P(B)$ when $\omega\in B$, and zero otherwise. The event after the bar is the known condition and supplies the denominator. If $P(B)=0$, this event-ratio definition is undefined.

## Mechanics: retain, total, normalize

The stage separates original event mass from conditional mass. Changing B changes which outcomes survive and the retained total. Changing A selects which of those survivors answer the target question. The original weights continue to define the experiment; the conditional view is derived from them.

Follow the trace: find $A\cap B$, add its original masses, find the original mass of B, and divide. The equation and inspector make the denominator visible. Replay after a membership change to explain why the same numerator can produce a different conditional answer.

## Derivation: why divide by P(B)?

Suppose every surviving weight is multiplied by a common factor $c$. We require a normalized conditional space, so

$$
\sum_{\omega\in B}cp_\omega=cP(B)=1.
$$

Thus $c=1/P(B)$, which exists only for positive retained mass. Summing these normalized weights over the target survivors gives the conditional formula. Rearranging also gives

$$
P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B),\qquad P(B)>0.
$$

This multiplication rule does not assume independence. It pairs the probability of the evidence with the chance of the target after that evidence.

## Worked examples

For the canonical die, $P(A\cap B)=2/6=1/3$ and $P(B)=3/6=1/2$. Therefore

$$
P(A\mid B)=\frac{1/3}{1/2}=\frac23.
$$

Within B, faces 4, 5, and 6 each have conditional mass $1/3$. Two of them belong to A. Knowing B increases the chance of A from $1/2$ to $2/3$, so these events are dependent.

For masses $p_x=1/2$, $p_y=1/3$, $p_z=1/6$, condition on $B=\{y,z\}$. Its mass is $1/2$, so the new masses are $2/3$ and $1/3$. Restricting to two outcomes does not make them equally likely.

Direction matters too. On a fair die, let $C=\{2\}$ and $D=\{2,4,6\}$. Then $P(C\mid D)=1/3$, but $P(D\mid C)=1$. The shared numerator is the same; the two denominators differ.

## Explore

Keep A fixed and shrink B to $\{4,6\}$: the conditional probability becomes one. Change B to $\{5\}$: it becomes zero. Set B to the whole space: conditioning changes nothing. Finally empty B: no conditional distribution exists.

Compare each case with the numerator and denominator separately. A zero numerator with positive denominator is a valid zero answer. A zero denominator prevents normalization entirely. You can perform these comparisons through membership controls and replay the derivation without waiting for random observations.

<Callout kind="scope" title="Impossible evidence is undefined, not zero">
An empty B has zero mass. A nonempty B containing only zero-weight outcomes also has zero mass. In both cases the formula would give 0/0, so the program must report undefined conditioning rather than return zero, one, or the original prior.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Keeping the original denominator computes the joint event, not the conditional probability. Swapping the events after and before the bar changes the question. Neither conditioning nor changing a diagram establishes causation: learning an event occurred is not the same operation as intervening to force it.

Another mistake is overwriting the original mass vector with a conditional one and then treating it as the original model in later calculations. Retain the base experiment so marginal, joint, and conditional quantities stay comparable.

## Numerical considerations

Exact fractions preserve the denominator-zero distinction. Small positive evidence is still valid evidence, not impossible evidence merely because its displayed decimal rounds to zero. However, division by a small evidence mass can amplify absolute input error, especially if the weights are measurements rather than exact specified values.

The NumPy reference checks small known answers within absolute tolerance $10^{-12}$ and zero relative tolerance. This is a reference-comparison tolerance, not a rule for replacing tiny positive evidence with zero. Larger inference systems may need scaled or logarithmic weights to avoid underflow; such numerical choices must keep impossible evidence distinct from numerical loss of representable mass.

## Engineering and ML connections

Conditional summaries compare data inside an observed subgroup. Their denominator is subgroup size or subgroup weight, not full dataset size. Conditional models likewise describe predictions given inputs or context. A selection filter can change rates even when the original population is unchanged, so always name the conditioned population before interpreting a metric.
