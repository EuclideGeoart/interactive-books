## Intuition: location does not specify spread

Two distributions can share a mean and have different spreads. A constant at $2$ and a fair choice between $0$ and $4$ both have mean $2$, but only the latter varies. We need a quantity that measures distance from the center rather than distance from zero.

Signed distances average to zero: negative deviations cancel positive ones. Squaring makes every distance contribution nonnegative and gives larger deviations greater influence. Weighting those squares by probability produces the variance; taking its nonnegative square root returns a spread in the original units.

## Definition

Write $\mu=\mathbb E[X]$. The **variance** and **standard deviation** are

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^2]
=\sum_i p_i(x_i-\mu)^2,
\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}.
$$

Variance is nonnegative. In a finite model it is zero precisely when every positive-mass value equals the mean; arbitrary zero-mass labels do not change it. If $X$ has units of milliseconds, variance has units of milliseconds squared, while standard deviation has units of milliseconds.

Finite models have finite moments. More generally, finite second moment guarantees a finite first moment and variance; distributions without a finite second moment require a separate discussion of infinite or undefined quantities.

## Derivation: the second-moment identity and affine law

Expand the centered square and use linearity:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2]
=\mathbb E[X^2]-2\mu\mathbb E[X]+\mu^2
=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mu^2.
$$

For $Z=aX+b$, its mean is $a\mu+b$, so $Z-\mathbb E[Z]=a(X-\mu)$. Squaring gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X),
\qquad \sigma_{aX+b}=|a|\sigma_X.
$$

A shift changes location and leaves spread unchanged. A negative scale reverses the number line but cannot make variance or standard deviation negative. With scale zero, all values collapse to the shift and the spread is zero.

## Worked examples

For the canonical model, $\mu=1/2$ and $\mathbb E[X^2]=5$. The signed deviations are $-5/2,-1/2,7/2$, whose squared values are $25/4,1/4,49/4$. Weighting them gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac14\frac{25}4+\frac12\frac14+\frac14\frac{49}4
=\frac{25}{16}+\frac2{16}+\frac{49}{16}
=\frac{19}4=5-\left(\frac12\right)^2.
$$

Thus $\sigma=\sqrt{19}/2\approx2.17944947177$. The value $4$ contributes the largest weighted square, $49/16$, even though its mass is only $1/4$.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

The second moment measures squares about zero, while variance measures squares about the mean. For a constant $X=2$, $\mathbb E[X^2]=4$ but variance is zero. Adding a constant also does not add its square to the variance: centering removes the same shift from every value.

Standard deviation is the nonnegative square root, not half the variance. For the canonical model it is $\sqrt{19}/2$, not $19/2$. A negative affine scale uses $a^2$ for variance and $|a|$ for standard deviation.

## Numerical considerations

The exact identity $\mathbb E[X^2]-\mu^2$ is convenient algebraically. With large offsets and small spread, floating-point subtraction can lose significant digits or produce a tiny negative result. Centered weighted squares avoid that particular subtraction. Extremely large values can still overflow floating-point squares; finite input alone does not guarantee a representable second moment.

The stage uses a specified theoretical model, so normalize its raw weights by their total. For $m>1$ independent identically distributed observed samples with an unknown mean, the usual unbiased **sample variance estimator** is

$$
s^2=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^m(x_j-\bar x)^2.
$$

This correction concerns estimation from observations, not the number of support labels or the raw model weight total. With one sample, that estimator is undefined. NumPy's default `var` uses `ddof=0`; `ddof=1` selects the usual sample denominator when its assumptions apply. Even then, the square root is not generally an unbiased estimator of population standard deviation.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Model weights are not automatically a sample">
The canonical weights [1, 2, 1] define probabilities [1/4, 1/2, 1/4]. Replacing their total 4 by 3 silently changes the requested theoretical variance. A sample estimator is a different quantity and needs an observed-data interpretation.
</Callout>

## Engineering and ML connections

Standardizing a feature uses its center and a positive spread scale. A zero-variance feature cannot be divided by its standard deviation and needs an explicit handling rule. Measurement-unit changes explain the squared scale law: converting milliseconds to seconds rescales variance by the conversion factor squared. Estimated data statistics and theoretical model moments should be labeled separately.
