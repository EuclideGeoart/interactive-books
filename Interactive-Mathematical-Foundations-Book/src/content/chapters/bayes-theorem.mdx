The example is a fictional item-quality flag, not a real diagnostic or medical recommendation. Before changing the prior, predict whether a flag rate of 90% among defective items implies that 90% of flagged items are defective.

## Intuition: evidence comes from both groups

A flag can be produced by a defective item or a nondefective item. If defective items are rare, the large nondefective group can contribute more flags even when its own flag rate is small. Updating a belief requires comparing these two sources of evidence.

Begin with prior mass in each group. Multiply each by how likely that group is to produce the observed evidence. Their retained masses need not sum to one: their total is the probability of the evidence. Divide by that total to compare the groups among items that match the evidence.

## Definition

Let A mean “the item is defective” and E mean “the item is flagged.” Write the **prior** $p=P(A)$, the flag **likelihood** $t=P(E\mid A)$, and the other flag likelihood $f=P(E\mid A^c)$. All three parameters lie in $[0,1]$.

The positive-evidence masses are $pt$ and $(1-p)f$. The evidence probability is their sum, and Bayes' theorem gives

$$
P(E)=pt+(1-p)f,\qquad
P(A\mid E)=\frac{pt}{pt+(1-p)f},\qquad P(E)>0.
$$

The **posterior** is the class probability after observing E. The false-positive rate is $f$ and the false-negative rate is $1-t$, relative to this fictional class model. A likelihood conditions on the class; a posterior conditions on the evidence. They have different denominators.

## Mechanics: prior, likelihood, retained mass, posterior

The stage's canonical parameters specify one joint model. Its mass view, equations, inspector, population table, accessible interpretation, and trace follow that model and the selected evidence. Changing the prior changes group sizes; changing a likelihood changes the retained fraction within its group.

Use the evidence selector to switch to no flag. The likelihoods become $1-t$ and $1-f$, and the retained total becomes $P(E^c)$. Replay the trace to follow the same update with the new evidence. Normalized posterior masses sum to one whenever the selected evidence has positive probability.

## Derivation: Bayes from the joint mass

The multiplication rule computes the same intersection in two ways:

$$
P(A\cap E)=P(A)P(E\mid A)=P(E)P(A\mid E).
$$

The two classes A and $A^c$ partition the sample space. Their evidence contributions therefore add to $P(E)$, which supplies the denominator. For negative evidence,

$$
P(A\mid E^c)=\frac{p(1-t)}{p(1-t)+(1-p)(1-f)},
\qquad P(E^c)>0.
$$

The joint model conserves original total mass: $pt+p(1-t)+(1-p)f+(1-p)(1-f)=1$. Restriction retains only the two evidence-compatible cells; normalization rescales them to total one. It does not change the original four cells.

## Worked examples

Use prior $p=1/100$, $t=9/10$, and $f=1/20$. In a notional population of $N=10000$ items, the **expected** counts are:

| Class | Flag E | No flag | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Defective A | 90 | 10 | 100 |
| Nondefective | 495 | 9405 | 9900 |
| Total | 585 | 9415 | 10000 |

These counts scale model probabilities; they are not a simulated sample or a promise that an actual batch contains those numbers. Other parameter choices can produce fractional expected counts, which should remain fractional rather than be silently rounded to people or items.

$$
P(E)=\frac{585}{10000}=\frac{117}{2000},\qquad
P(A\mid E)=\frac{90}{585}=\frac2{13}\approx0.153846.
$$

With no flag, the defective share is $10/(10+9405)=2/1883\approx0.001062$. It is not $1-2/13$; that complement is the nondefective share **among flagged items**. Raising the prior to $1/10$ with the same positive likelihoods changes the posterior to $(9/100)/(9/100+9/200)=2/3$. Base rates matter even when the flag mechanism does not change.

## Explore

Set both likelihoods equal and positive. Positive evidence then retains the same proportion of each class, leaving the posterior equal to the prior. If both are strictly less than one, the same is true after negative evidence. Next set the false-positive rate to zero with positive prior and positive true-flag likelihood: every possible flag comes from A, so its posterior is one.

Set both positive likelihoods to zero and choose flag evidence. The evidence is impossible and the posterior must be undefined. Set both to one and choose no flag for the parallel case. Try prior zero or one and identify when the selected evidence is still possible under the surviving class.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Confusing $P(E\mid A)$ with $P(A\mid E)$ reverses the conditioning direction. Ignoring the nondefective evidence contribution commits the base-rate fallacy. Using the false-negative rate as the negative-evidence posterior also skips the denominator.

A flag is evidence under an assumed model, not certainty about an individual item or proof of a cause. The fictional rates are teaching parameters, not empirically calibrated quality guarantees. An observed count table would need its own sampling and estimation analysis before its rates could be treated as known.

## Numerical considerations

Finite rational parameters give exact joint masses, expected counts, evidence totals, and posteriors. Decimal displays with $\approx$ are approximations of these exact quantities. The NumPy reference uses absolute tolerance $10^{-12}$ and zero relative tolerance for these small specified examples, rather than asserting binary-decimal identity.

Very small but positive evidence remains mathematically valid; division may expose input uncertainty or floating-point underflow. A general numerical implementation may need scaling or log-space normalization, but must disclose that policy and preserve the distinction between impossible evidence and arithmetic loss. Adding epsilon to an impossible denominator changes the model and does not derive Bayes' theorem.

## Engineering and ML connections

Bayesian classification updates class priors with feature likelihoods. Changing deployment base rates can change posterior predictions even if the class-conditional likelihoods stay fixed. This is one reason a detection rate alone is insufficient to interpret flagged cases. Practical models must justify their likelihoods and priors; the algebra faithfully updates assumptions but cannot certify those assumptions.
