## Intuition: size depends on what you measure

Euclidean length measures a straight move. Taxicab length counts the total travel along the two coordinate axes. The largest-component norm measures the worst coordinate displacement. These answer different questions about the same vector. None depends on which quadrant contains it, because signs describe direction rather than size.

Normalization removes Euclidean length without changing a nonzero vector's direction. A short vector and a long vector along the same ray give the same normalized arrow. A vector on the opposite ray gives the negative unit vector. The unit circle acts as a catalogue of possible directions.

## Definition

For $\mathbf v=(v_x,v_y)$,

$$
\norm{\mathbf v}_2=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2},\qquad
\norm{\mathbf v}_1=|v_x|+|v_y|,\qquad
\norm{\mathbf v}_\infty=\max(|v_x|,|v_y|).
$$

An unqualified norm in this block means the Euclidean norm. A **unit vector** has Euclidean norm $1$. The normalized vector is defined only for $\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0$:

$$
\widehat{\mathbf v}=\frac{\mathbf v}{\norm{\mathbf v}_2}.
$$

A norm is nonnegative, vanishes only at zero, scales by the absolute value of a scalar, and satisfies the triangle inequality $\norm{\mathbf u+\mathbf v}\le\norm{\mathbf u}+\norm{\mathbf v}$. For Euclidean distance, the straight route is no longer than the route through an intermediate point. Equality occurs for moves along the same ray, including a zero move.

## Mechanics: the three unit balls

The **unit ball** of a norm consists of vectors whose norm is at most $1$. The stage draws its boundary. For $L_1$, $|x|+|y|=1$ gives the diamond. For $L_\infty$, $\max(|x|,|y|)=1$ gives the square. For $L_2$, $x^2+y^2=1$ gives the circle. These shapes follow directly from the norm definitions, rather than from the current vector's length.

At $(1,1)$ the norms are $2$, $\sqrt2$, and $1$, respectively. The point lies on the square boundary, outside the Euclidean circle, and outside the diamond. If you divide by $L_1$ instead, the resulting vector lands on the diamond; it generally is not a Euclidean unit vector.

## Derivation: why the normalized length is one

The length is positive when the vector is nonzero. Apply the scaling law from <Xref to="chapters/vector-scaling/">II.2 Scalar multiplication</Xref>:

$$
\norm{\widehat{\mathbf v}}_2
=\left|\frac1{\norm{\mathbf v}_2}\right|\norm{\mathbf v}_2=1.
$$

The scaling factor is positive, so the direction is preserved. At zero the factor would be $1/0$, and this derivation cannot begin. A zero result is not a substitute: its norm is $0$, not $1$.

The component inequalities also compare the three norms:

$$
\norm{\mathbf v}_\infty\le\norm{\mathbf v}_2\le\norm{\mathbf v}_1.
$$

The first follows because the sum of squares contains the square of the larger component. For the second, $(|x|+|y|)^2=x^2+y^2+2|xy|\ge x^2+y^2$; taking nonnegative square roots preserves the inequality. Both become equalities on an axis. The ordering does not make one norm universally better; each defines a different size measure.

## Common mistakes

Dividing by the squared norm is generally wrong: $(3,4)/25$ has length $1/5$. Dividing each component by its own magnitude gives a sign pattern, not a unit direction. Adding a tiny constant to the denominator can avoid a software exception but changes the formula and produces lengths below $1$; such regularization needs its own explicit purpose and definition.

## Numerical considerations

Squaring first can overflow or underflow even when a meaningful unit direction exists. For finite $\mathbf v\ne0$, set $m=\max(|v_x|,|v_y|)$ and $\mathbf z=\mathbf v/m$. At least one scaled component has magnitude $1$, and neither exceeds $1$. Then normalize $\mathbf z$ by its bounded length. This works for enormous inputs and for a pair of smallest subnormal components. The real norm itself may exceed the largest finite binary64 value; direction can still be representable.

<Callout kind="numerical" title="Stability cannot restore a lost input">
Rescaling protects a representable nonzero input. If earlier arithmetic already rounded both components to zero, normalization must report zero's exception. It cannot recover the original direction. A measured vector very near zero may also have a poorly determined direction even when the computation is stable.
</Callout>

## Engineering and ML connections

Unit directions separate where a motion goes from how far it travels. Different norms measure different costs: total coordinate movement, Euclidean displacement, or a worst coordinate limit. Feature normalization can reduce the influence of overall scale in a similarity calculation, but discarding magnitude also discards information. Choose it because the task needs direction, rather than because every vector should have unit length.
