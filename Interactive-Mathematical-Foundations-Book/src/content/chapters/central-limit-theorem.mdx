## Intuition: study many means, not one running path

The previous chapter followed a single growing average. Here we repeatedly draw blocks of the same size $n$ and calculate one mean per block. Their collection has a distribution of its own. The source can remain skewed while the standardized mean distribution becomes more nearly Gaussian as $n$ grows.

A raw mean's spread shrinks with $n$. To compare shape across block sizes, remove its theoretical center and divide by its theoretical standard deviation. This standardization keeps the reference spread fixed; it does not force observed bars to match a normal curve.

## Definition

Let $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be iid with finite mean $\mu$ and finite positive variance $\sigma^2$. For the mean of a block of size $n$, define

$$
Z_n=\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}
=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^nX_j-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}.
$$

The classical **Central Limit Theorem** states $Z_n\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1)$. Convergence in distribution means that, at every real $z$ here, $\mathbb P(Z_n\leq z)$ tends to the standard-normal CDF $\Phi(z)$. It does not make finite-$n$ observations exactly Gaussian or specify a universal accuracy threshold such as $n=30$.

If $\sigma^2=0$, the source is constant almost surely and this standardization is undefined. A normal overlay must not be presented as a valid standardized limit in that state.

## Derivation: why the center and scale take this form

Under iid assumptions, linearity and zero cross-covariances give

$$
\mathbb E[\bar X_n]=\mu,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}.
$$

Subtracting $\mu$ centers the mean, and division by $\sigma/\sqrt n$ gives $\mathbb E[Z_n]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Z_n)=1$. These moment identities explain the scaling but do not prove the Gaussian limit. Many nonnormal distributions also have mean zero and variance one; convergence in distribution is the additional theorem.

An equivalent sum formulation subtracts $n\mu$ and divides by $\sigma\sqrt n$. Confusing sum and mean scales changes the variance of the standardized result and can create a misleadingly narrow or wide histogram.

## Worked examples

Four rate-$1$ exponential observations with sum $6$ have mean $3/2$. Their population mean and standard deviation are both $1$, so

$$
Z_4=\sqrt4\left(\frac32-1\right)=1.
$$

This is one standardized mean, not a proof about its distribution. For Bernoulli probability $1/4$ and $n=100$, the repeated means have theoretical expectation $1/4$ and variance $3/1600$.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

The LLN concerns a mean approaching its target; the CLT concerns the distribution of its centered, rescaled fluctuations. Neither says the original observations become Gaussian. The population plot remains a separate object throughout the experiment.

Perfect within-block dependence breaks the iid variance calculation. If every entry equals $Y$, then $\bar X_n=Y$ and the iid reference $\sqrt n(Y-\mu)/\sigma$ has variance $n$, not $1$. An iid Cauchy population violates the classical theorem's finite-variance and finite-mean assumptions; averaging it does not justify this Gaussian standardization.

There is no universal finite sample size at which every source becomes “normal enough.” Tail behavior, skew, event of interest, and desired accuracy matter. One seeded histogram supplies an illustration rather than evidence that all theorem assumptions hold or that an asymptotic approximation is accurate for a particular application.

## Numerical considerations

The stage uses $24$ equal-width bins on $[-4,4)$, each of width $1/3$. Values below $-4$ and values at or above $4$ are counted separately. Bin density is

$$
h_k=\frac{c_k}{R\Delta},\qquad
\sum_kh_k\Delta=\frac{R_{\mathrm{inside}}}{R},
$$

where $R$ is all repetitions and $\Delta$ is bin width. The histogram area is the retained empirical fraction, not automatically one. The analytic standard-normal reference is normalized over the entire real line and has positive tail mass outside every finite window; its omitted mass beyond $\pm4$ is approximately $0.000063342484$.

The normal overlay uses the Gaussian density and CDF policy from the preceding distribution chapter; CDF approximations have a stated absolute error budget of $10^{-9}$, so a difference has budget $2\times10^{-9}$. This numerical error is separate from Monte Carlo noise, finite-$n$ approximation error, binning, finite drawing bounds, and pseudo-random stream limitations. None is removed by aligning plotted heights manually.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Repetitions estimate a shape; n changes the shape">
For a Bernoulli source at n=1, a million repetitions still estimate a two-point distribution. Increasing n supplies more independent observations to each mean. Increasing repetitions supplies more examples of the current mean distribution.
</Callout>

## Engineering and ML connections

The CLT motivates approximate Gaussian descriptions of accumulated independent finite-variance noise and some Monte Carlo estimation errors. The raw average's standard-deviation scale is $\sigma/\sqrt n$; using it requires checking the dependence and moment assumptions, and the approximation's usefulness depends on the event being estimated.

Batch averages in learning systems often contain correlated observations, and heavy-tailed quantities can violate classical assumptions. The normal reference is a model to assess, not a consequence of drawing a smooth curve over data. Statistical confidence procedures build additional decisions on this probability foundation in the next part of the book.
