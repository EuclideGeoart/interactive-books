## Intuition: a center of probability mass

Expectation is a weighted balance point, not a guarantee about the next result. Imagine each value carrying its probability mass on a number line. A larger mass contributes more strongly to the center. Negative values pull in the negative direction; they do not require negative probabilities.

For values $[-2,0,4]$ and raw weights $[1,2,1]$, the normalized masses are $[1/4,1/2,1/4]$. Their center is $1/2$, even though no outcome takes that value. The ordinary average of the three labels, $2/3$, describes a different equal-weight model.

## Definition

For a finite random variable with values $x_i$ and probabilities $p_i$, its **expectation** is

$$
\mathbb E[X]=\sum_i p_i x_i.
$$

For a function $g$, $\mathbb E[g(X)]=\sum_i p_i g(x_i)$. In particular, the **second moment** is $\mathbb E[X^2]=\sum_i p_i x_i^2$; it is generally different from $(\mathbb E[X])^2$.

For a continuous variable with density $f$, $\mathbb E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f(x)\,dx$ when the expectation exists. A finite absolute first moment ensures this definition is well behaved; not every infinite-support distribution has one. The finite models in this stage always do.

## Derivation: linearity does not need independence

For constants $a,b$,

$$
\mathbb E[aX+b]=\sum_i p_i(ax_i+b)
=a\mathbb E[X]+b\sum_i p_i=a\mathbb E[X]+b.
$$

For two jointly defined finite variables, sum over their common outcomes. Distributing the sum gives $\mathbb E[X+Y]=\mathbb E[X]+\mathbb E[Y]$. Independence is unnecessary: both values can depend on the same outcome. In general settings these identities require the relevant expectations to exist.

An event indicator $I_A$ is $1$ on $A$ and $0$ elsewhere, so $\mathbb E[I_A]=P(A)$. A binomial count is a sum of Bernoulli indicators; linearity gives mean $np$. Independence is required for its binomial distribution, but not for the expectation of a sum of indicators with given marginal success probabilities.

## Worked examples

The canonical weighted calculation is

$$
\mathbb E[X]=\frac{-2+2\times0+4}{4}=\frac12,
\qquad \mathbb E[X^2]=\frac{4+2\times0+16}{4}=5.
$$

Define $Y=2X+1$ on the same outcome. This is a dependent pair, not a second independent draw. Its values are $[-3,1,9]$, with the same masses. Since $\mathbb E[Y]=2\mathbb E[X]+1=2$,

$$
\mathbb E[X+Y]=3\mathbb E[X]+1=\frac52.
$$

By contrast, $XY=2X^2+X$ gives $\mathbb E[XY]=21/2$, whereas $\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]=1$. Linearity concerns sums and constant multiples, not arbitrary products.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

The mean need not be a possible value or the most probable one. Here the most probable value is $0$, while the mean is $1/2$. Rounding the mean to a support point changes the quantity rather than correcting it.

Squaring before averaging differs from squaring afterward: $\mathbb E[X^2]=5$ but $(\mathbb E[X])^2=1/4$. Also, independence is not a prerequisite for linearity. The explicit $Y=2X+1$ example is dependent and still obeys the sum rule. Its product calculation refutes a careless extension of that rule.

## Numerical considerations

Finite rational values and weights allow exact expectations. Construct fractions from integers or decimal strings rather than first rounding them to binary floats. NumPy calculations are approximate in general; exact-looking canonical decimals do not imply every weighted input remains exact.

A **sample mean** averages observed data, whereas this stage computes a **theoretical expectation** from a specified model. Raw weights describe proportional probabilities here, not necessarily sample frequencies. Agreement between a short observed average and the model mean is not guaranteed; the sampling chapters study that distinction later.

<Callout kind="scope" title="Existence matters beyond finite models">
Linearity applies when the relevant expectations exist. A symmetric infinite-support density does not by symmetry alone prove that its expectation exists; divergent positive and negative contributions cannot be cancelled by declaration.
</Callout>

## Engineering and ML connections

Expected loss weights per-outcome losses by the model's probabilities. Combining loss terms uses linearity even when they depend on the same example. Replacing theoretical probabilities with observed frequencies yields an empirical average; it estimates a model quantity under additional assumptions rather than becoming an exact expectation automatically.
