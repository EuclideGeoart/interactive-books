## Intuition: how the slope itself changes

The gradient says which way is uphill. Second derivatives say how that slope changes as you move — how the surface **curves**. Along a direction $\mathbf u$, the curvature of the slice $t\mapsto f(p+t\mathbf u)$ at $t=0$ is a number; positive means the slice bends upward. A minimum curves upward in every direction, a maximum downward in every direction, and a saddle upward in some directions and downward in others.

## Definition

The **Hessian** of $f$ at $p$ is the matrix of second partial derivatives,

$$
H=\begin{bmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\ f_{yx}&f_{yy}\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For smooth functions (and all polynomials) the mixed partials agree, $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$, so $H$ is symmetric. The curvature along a unit vector $\mathbf u$ is $\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf u$; its extreme values are the eigenvalues of $H$, the **principal curvatures**, along the eigenvector directions.

**Second-derivative test.** At a critical point ($\nabla f=\mathbf 0$): if $\det H>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$, a strict local minimum; if $\det H>0$ and $f_{xx}<0$, a strict local maximum; if $\det H<0$, a saddle; if $\det H=0$, the test is inconclusive.

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

All second partials, the determinant and the trace are exact; both mixed partials are computed separately and compared. The classification is an exact rational decision. Principal curvatures are binary64 roots from the exact trace and discriminant. The right plane draws contours of the **quadratic model** $Q(\boldsymbol\delta)=f(p)+\nabla f\cdot\boldsymbol\delta+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^{\mathsf T}H\boldsymbol\delta$ with the same levels as $f$. If the point is not critical, the stage says that the test does not apply.

## Derivation: why the determinant decides

Near a critical point $f(p+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx f(p)+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^{\mathsf T}H\boldsymbol\delta$. For a symmetric $H$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ and orthonormal eigenvectors, writing $\boldsymbol\delta$ in that basis gives

$$
\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^{\mathsf T}H\boldsymbol\delta=\tfrac12(\lambda_1c_1^2+\lambda_2c_2^2).
$$

If both eigenvalues are positive the quadratic is positive for every $\boldsymbol\delta\ne\mathbf 0$ (minimum); both negative, a maximum; opposite signs, a saddle. Since $\det H=\lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{tr}H=\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, the sign of $\det H$ tells whether the signs agree, and $f_{xx}$ (or the trace) tells which. If $\det H=0$ some eigenvalue is zero, the quadratic is flat in that direction, and higher-order terms decide.

## Worked example

$f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2$ has $\nabla f=(3x^2-3,\,2y)$, so its critical points are $(\pm1,0)$. The Hessian is

$$
H=\begin{bmatrix}6x&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

At $(1,0)$: $\det H=12>0$, $f_{xx}=6>0$ — a minimum. At $(-1,0)$: $\det H=-12<0$ — a saddle.

## When the test is inconclusive

For $x^4+y^4$, $x^2+y^3$ and the monkey saddle $x^3-3xy^2$, the origin is critical and $\det H=0$. The first has a minimum, the second takes negative values along the negative $y$-axis and positive values along the $x$-axis, and the monkey saddle goes down in three directions and up in three. Same Hessian test result, three different shapes: second derivatives alone cannot decide, and the quadratic model on the right is visibly degenerate.

## Common mistakes

Applying the test at a point where $\nabla f\ne\mathbf 0$. Concluding "minimum" or "maximum" when $\det H=0$. Using only $f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ and forgetting the mixed term $f_{xy}$, which rotates the principal directions.

## Numerical considerations

Deciding $\det H=0$ in floating point is a tolerance choice, not a fact: nearly singular Hessians make classification sensitive to rounding. The explorer decides exactly because its inputs are rational. Numerically, eigenvalues of symmetric matrices are well conditioned, but the eigenvectors of nearly repeated eigenvalues are not.

## Engineering and ML connections

Newton's method for optimisation solves $H\boldsymbol\delta=-\nabla f$; curvature also explains why gradient descent zig-zags in narrow valleys and how step sizes relate to the largest eigenvalue (VI.5). In high dimensions most critical points of loss landscapes are saddles. This chapter supplies the vocabulary; the optimisation algorithms come later.

## Summary and checkpoints

The Hessian collects second partial derivatives and is symmetric for smooth functions. At a critical point its determinant and diagonal classify minima, maxima and saddles; when $\det H=0$ the test is inconclusive. The quadratic model is the local second-order picture.
