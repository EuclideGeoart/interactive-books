## Intuition: shape first

Most of the functions an engineer or a machine-learning practitioner meets are built from six simple shapes. Learn the shapes first; the formulas then explain them.

- **The line**, $y=x$. Steady change: each step right adds the same amount. Its graph is straight.
- **The parabola**, $y=x^{2}$. Change that speeds up away from the bottom. It is symmetric: $x$ and $-x$ give the same value, so squaring forgets signs.
- **The exponential**, $y=e^{x}$. Change in proportion to the amount already there. It is always positive, it never reaches the axis, and on the right it grows faster than any line or parabola, although not immediately.
- **The logarithm**, $y=\ln x$. The exponential read backwards: it asks *what power of $e$ gives $x$?* It exists only for $x>0$, falls without bound as $x$ approaches $0$, and climbs more and more slowly to the right.
- **The sine and the cosine**, $y=\sin x$ and $y=\cos x$. A wave that repeats every $2\pi$ and never leaves the band from $-1$ to $1$. The cosine is the sine started a quarter of a period earlier.

Here $e\approx 2.718282$ is the number for which the exponential has the pleasant property that its steepness at any point equals its height there. The book writes $\approx$ because $e$ is irrational and the digits are a rounding; the stage above uses the same rule, and it writes $=$ only for values that are exactly known.

## Definition

All six share one pattern. Take a basic function $g$ from the list and two numbers $a$ and $h$:

$$
y=a\,g(x-h),\qquad g\in\{\,x,\ x^{2},\ e^{x},\ \ln x,\ \sin x,\ \cos x\,\}.
$$

The number $a$ is the **vertical scale**: it stretches the graph away from the $x$-axis, and a negative $a$ also flips it upside down. The number $h$ is the **horizontal shift**: the graph of $g(x-h)$ is the graph of $g$ moved *right* by $h$, which is the opposite of what the minus sign tempts you to expect (the value $g$ had at $0$ is now found at $x=h$).

Every function here has a **domain**, the inputs for which it exists, and a **range**, the outputs it can produce. These come from $g$ and the shift, and they are the first thing to know:

- $x$ and $x^{2}$ and $e^{x}$ and $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ accept every real number; $\ln x$ accepts only $x>0$, so $a\ln(x-h)$ exists only for $x>h$, with a vertical asymptote at $x=h$.
- The range of $a\sin(x-h)$ is the interval from $-|a|$ to $|a|$. The range of $a\,e^{x-h}$ is the positive numbers when $a>0$ and the negative numbers when $a<0$. The range of $a\ln(x-h)$ is every real number.
- If $a=0$ the result is the constant $0$ (where it exists), whichever $g$ you chose. The stage flags that as a special case, because the shape disappears.

<Callout kind="scope" title="What the parameters leave out">
This family has two knobs, $a$ and $h$. A line through $(h,0)$ with slope $a$ is a line, but only a line through the $x$-axis; $y=a(x-h)$ cannot be a horizontal line other than $y=0$. Adding a vertical shift $k$ (so $y=a\,g(x-h)+k$) or a frequency (as in $\sin 3x$) changes nothing in the reasoning below and is left out to keep the picture readable.
</Callout>

An exponential has a small surprise hidden in this pattern: shifting it and scaling it are the *same* operation, because $a\,e^{x-h}=(a\,e^{-h})\,e^{x}$. Slide $h$ and watch the curve; then change $a$ to reach the identical curve. No other family does this.

## Mechanics: shifts, scales and inverses

### Shifts and scales

Use the stage at the top (or the full copy in the **Explore** section) to see the two parameters act. Set only the sine on and raise $a$ to $3$: the range becomes $[-3,3]$ and the zeros do not move, because a vertical stretch never moves the points where a function is $0$. Raise $h$ instead and the zeros slide.

### Inverse pairs and their honest domains

Two functions are **inverses** when each undoes the other. The exponential and the logarithm are one such pair, and squaring and the square root are another. On a graph, inverses are mirror images across the line $y=x$, because undoing a function swaps the roles of input and output, and swapping coordinates is exactly that mirror.

The honest statement of the pair needs the domains:

$$
\ln\!\left(e^{x}\right)=x\ \text{ for every real }x,\qquad e^{\ln x}=x\ \text{ only for }x>0 .
$$

The first holds everywhere because $e^{x}$ is defined and positive for every $x$. The second needs $x>0$ because $\ln x$ does not exist otherwise. The square-and-root pair has the same trap in a different place: $\sqrt{x^{2}}=|x|$, which equals $x$ only when $x\ge0$, and $\left(\sqrt{x}\right)^{2}=x$ only for $x\ge0$, since $\sqrt{x}$ does not exist for negative $x$.

### The unit circle

Trigonometry begins with one circle. The **unit circle** is the circle of radius $1$ centred at the origin. Start at $(1,0)$ and walk counter-clockwise a distance $\theta$ along the circle; the point you reach is, by definition,

$$
(\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta).
$$

The distance walked, $\theta$, is measured in **radians**: one full turn is $2\pi$ because the circumference of a circle of radius $1$ is $2\pi$. So $\pi$ is a half-turn, $\pi/2$ a quarter-turn, and $180^{\circ}=\pi$ radians. Walking clockwise is a negative angle, matching the convention used for vector directions in the vectors chapter. Walking a whole turn more brings you back to the same point, which is why both functions repeat: $\sin(\theta+2\pi)=\sin\theta$.

Because the point lies on the circle, its coordinates satisfy $\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=1$: it is Pythagoras applied to the right triangle with the radius as hypotenuse.

Two inverse functions go with the circle, and both have limited ranges. $\arcsin$ returns only angles from $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$, so $\arcsin(\sin\theta)=\theta$ only for those $\theta$; for $\theta=2$ it returns $\pi-2$, the other angle with the same sine. The two-argument function $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$, met in the vectors chapter, returns the angle in $(-\pi,\pi]$ from *both* coordinates, so it recovers $\theta$ up to whole turns. The verdict line under the stage says, for each angle, whether $\arcsin$ and $\operatorname{atan2}$ give the angle back.

### Changing the base

The exponential $e^{x}$ is one member of a family. For any base $b>0$ with $b\ne1$ we have $b^{x}=e^{x\ln b}$, so every exponential is the natural one with the input rescaled by the constant $\ln b$. The inverse of $b^{x}$ is the **logarithm to the base $b$**, written $\log_{b}x$: the power of $b$ that gives $x$. It exists for $x>0$ only.

Base $1$ is excluded because $1^{x}=1$ for every $x$: it is a constant, not an exponential, and its "logarithm" would have to answer *what power of $1$ gives $7$?* — nothing does. For a base between $0$ and $1$ the curve falls instead of rising (halving rather than doubling).

Any logarithm is a natural logarithm divided by a constant, which is how a computer evaluates all of them:

$$
\log_{b}x=\frac{\ln x}{\ln b}\qquad(x>0,\ b>0,\ b\ne1).
$$

The same constant gives the **doubling time**. For $b>1$ the quantity $b^{x}$ doubles every time $x$ grows by $\ln 2/\ln b$, and for $0<b<1$ it halves every $\ln 2/|\ln b|$. And it converts units of information: $\log_{2}x=\ln x/\ln 2$ counts **bits** and $\ln x$ counts **nats**.

Finally, four identities hold for positive numbers and are what make logarithms useful:

$$
\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b,\qquad \ln\!\left(a^{k}\right)=k\ln a,\qquad \log_{b}a=\frac{\ln a}{\ln b} .
$$

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="misconception" title="ln(a + b) is not ln a + ln b">
$\ln(2+3)=\ln 5\approx1.609$, but $\ln2+\ln3=\ln6\approx1.792$. The logarithm turns *products* into sums: $\ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b$. It does nothing useful with a sum inside.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="The square root undoes the square">
Only for non-negative numbers. $\sqrt{(-7)^{2}}=\sqrt{49}=7$, not $-7$: squaring maps both $7$ and $-7$ to $49$, so no function can recover the sign.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Degrees go into sin and cos">
`math.sin(30)` is not $1/2$. It is the sine of $30$ *radians*, which is about $-0.988$. Convert first with `math.radians(30)`, or better, work in radians throughout.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="arcsin(sin x) = x">
Only for $x$ between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$. For $x=2$ you get $\pi-2\approx1.1416$, the other angle with the same sine. A sine value of $1/2$ is shared by *two* angles in every turn, and $\arcsin$ reports just one.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="An exponential beats a power straight away">
It wins *eventually*, not immediately. $2^{x}$ and $x^{2}$ are equal at $x=2$ and $x=4$, and in between $x^{2}$ is larger ($2^{3}=8<9$). After $x=4$ the exponential never looks back.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="A positive shift moves the graph left">
$g(x-h)$ moves the graph of $g$ to the *right* by $h$. The value that used to occur at $x=0$ now occurs at $x=h$. To see it, check where $\ln(x-h)$ has its zero: at $x=h+1$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="log means the same thing everywhere">
In Python `math.log` and `np.log` are the **natural** logarithm; `math.log10` and `np.log10` are base $10$; `math.log2` is base $2$. Many textbooks write $\log$ for base $10$ and some for base $2$. State the base.
</Callout>

## Numerical considerations

The exponential has a narrow window in which a double can hold it. The largest double is about $1.8\times10^{308}$, so $e^{x}$ overflows just above $x=709.78$; the smallest positive double is about $5\times10^{-324}$, so $e^{x}$ underflows to exactly $0$ just below $x=-745.13$. The table is computed by the same model as the stages above.

<ExpLimitsTable />

When the stored value is $\infty$ or $0$, the stage writes $=$ because it is stating a fact about the *double* ("the stored value is $\infty$"), and it adds a note giving the truth: the real value is finite and large, or positive and tiny. Python and NumPy even disagree on what to do. `math.exp(710.0)` raises `OverflowError`; `np.exp(710.0)` quietly returns `inf`, and `inf / inf` is `nan`, which then poisons every number it touches.

<Callout kind="numerical">
Never compute $e^{x}$ for a large $x$ directly if you only need a ratio or a logarithm of a sum. Subtract the largest exponent first, so that every exponent is at most $0$ and the largest term is exactly $e^{0}=1$. Compare floating-point results with `math.isclose` (a tolerance), never `==`, and keep exact values (such as $\sqrt3/2$) as exact values until you must print them.
</Callout>

Three more places where the machine and the mathematics part ways. First, $e^{\ln x}$ is $x$ only up to rounding: `math.exp(math.log(10.0))` is `10.000000000000002`. Second, $\pi$ is stored rounded, so $\sin\pi$ is about $1.2\times10^{-16}$, not $0$, and $\cos(\pi/2)$ is about $6\times10^{-17}$. Third, `math.log(1000, 10)` is `2.9999999999999996` but `math.log10(1000.0)` is `3.0`: when a dedicated function for your base exists, use it.

## Connections to engineering and machine learning

- **Softmax and log-sum-exp.** A classifier turns scores $s_i$ into probabilities $e^{s_i}/\sum_j e^{s_j}$. With scores near $1000$ the exponentials overflow. Subtracting the largest score $m$ multiplies the top and the bottom by the same number $e^{-m}$, so the probabilities do not change, and every exponent becomes at most $0$. The same shift gives $\ln\sum_j e^{s_j}=m+\ln\sum_j e^{s_j-m}$, the standard stable form used in the loss functions of almost every framework.
- **Logarithms turn products into sums.** The likelihood of $1000$ independent events each with probability $0.01$ is $10^{-2000}$, which underflows to $0$ in a double. The log-likelihood is $1000\ln 0.01\approx-4605.17$, an ordinary number. This is why models are trained on the log-likelihood.
- **Bits and nats.** Cross-entropy and information are measured in nats when computed with $\ln$ (the default in most libraries) and in bits with $\log_2$; one nat is $1/\ln2\approx1.4427$ bits. Telling $1024$ equally likely cases apart takes $\log_21024=10$ bits.
- **Exponential growth and decay.** Compound growth, radioactive decay, learning-rate schedules and the attenuation of a signal are all $a\,b^{x}$; the doubling or halving time $\ln2/|\ln b|$ is the one number that describes them.
- **Sine and cosine.** Every periodic signal is built from them, and a rotation of the plane by $\theta$ is the matrix whose entries are $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$. Positional encodings in transformers use sines and cosines of many frequencies; all of them take radians.
- **The logarithm as a compressor.** On a log axis, multiplying a quantity by a fixed factor moves it a fixed distance. This is why spectra, learning curves and file sizes are plotted that way.

## Summary and checkpoints

- Six shapes carry most of the work: the line, the parabola, the exponential, the logarithm, the sine and the cosine. Each is $a\,g(x-h)$ for a basic $g$: $a$ stretches vertically, $h$ shifts right.
- Domains matter. $\ln$ exists only for positive inputs; $\sqrt{}$ only for non-negative ones. $\ln(e^{x})=x$ for every $x$, but $e^{\ln x}=x$ only for $x>0$, and $\sqrt{x^{2}}=|x|$.
- Inverse functions are mirror images across $y=x$.
- On the unit circle, $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ is the point at distance $\theta$ (radians) from $(1,0)$, and $\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=1$. $\arcsin$ returns only angles in $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$; $\operatorname{atan2}$ returns angles in $(-\pi,\pi]$.
- $\log_{b}x=\ln x/\ln b$; the doubling time is $\ln2/\ln b$; bits use base $2$, nats use base $e$.
- In code: radians everywhere, tolerances instead of `==`, shift exponents before using them, and expect `exp` to overflow near $709.78$.
