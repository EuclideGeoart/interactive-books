## Intuition: the table keeps the pairing

A marginal distribution describes one variable on its own. It does not say which values occur together. Two households can have the same distribution of daily electricity use and different relationships between morning and evening use. A joint table preserves those pairings.

Each cell represents one pair $(U,V)$. A row collects all pairs with a particular $U$; a column collects all pairs with a particular $V$. Summing a row removes the $V$ coordinate. Conditioning on that row instead keeps its internal proportions and rescales the retained mass to one.

## Definition

For finite variables, the joint PMF is

$$
p_{ij}=\mathbb P(U=u_i,V=v_j),\qquad
p_{ij}\geq0,\qquad\sum_i\sum_jp_{ij}=1.
$$

The **marginals** sum across the unobserved coordinate:

$$
p_U(u_i)=\sum_jp_{ij},\qquad p_V(v_j)=\sum_ip_{ij}.
$$

If $p_U(u_i)>0$, the **conditional PMF** of $V$ given $U=u_i$ is

$$
p_{V\mid U}(v_j\mid u_i)=\frac{p_{ij}}{p_U(u_i)}.
$$

The analogous column formula divides by $p_V(v_j)>0$. A zero marginal makes this finite conditioning formula undefined; it does not produce an all-zero or uniform distribution.

## Derivation: why the conditional row sums to one

For a positive-mass row, its conditional masses satisfy

$$
\sum_jp_{V\mid U}(v_j\mid u_i)
=\frac{\sum_jp_{ij}}{p_U(u_i)}=1.
$$

Multiplying the conditional formula back by its denominator gives the product rule

$$
p_{ij}=p_U(u_i)\,p_{V\mid U}(v_j\mid u_i).
$$

Independence requires every joint cell to factor:

$$
p_{ij}=p_U(u_i)p_V(v_j)\quad\text{for all }i,j.
$$

For positive conditioning rows, that equality means the conditional distribution equals the unconditional $V$ marginal. The cellwise test also handles zero rows: every cell there is zero, so both sides of the factorization are zero without dividing by zero.

## Worked examples

Both canonical marginals are $[1/4,1/2,1/4]$. The corner joint mass is $1/8$, while the product of its marginals is $(1/4)(1/4)=1/16$. These variables are dependent.

Condition on $U=-1$. Retained row weights $[1,1,0]$ have total $2$, so

$$
p_{V\mid U}(\cdot\mid-1)=\left[\frac12,\frac12,0\right].
$$

Conditioning on the middle row instead gives $[1/4,1/2,1/4]$, which happens to match the $V$ marginal. One matching row does not prove independence; the corner test already disproves it.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Summing and conditioning answer different questions. The canonical top-row marginal is $1/4$; its conditional distribution has total $1$. Keeping the global denominator in a conditional row would leave total $1/4$ and fail normalization.

Marginals do not determine the joint table. The canonical and independent tables have identical marginals but different corner masses. Also, checking only one row, one covariance, or one cell cannot establish independence across all cells. A mismatch in any cell does suffice to disprove it.

## Numerical considerations

In this bounded table, integer weights and normalized fractions support exact sums and comparisons. An all-zero total is rejected; individual zero rows and columns are preserved and reported explicitly. Display rounding never decides whether a conditional denominator is zero or whether the table factors.

With floating data, very small positive marginals can amplify relative errors during conditional division. Distinguish an exact zero from a small positive quantity, and document any numerical comparison tolerance. Do not round a valid row to zero before computing its conditional probabilities.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="An empty condition carries no finite conditional PMF">
A zero row has no retained mass to normalize. Replacing it with equal probabilities would add an assumption absent from the original model; replacing it with zeros would fail to define a probability distribution.
</Callout>

## Engineering and ML connections

A classification confusion matrix records joint behavior between a label and a prediction. Row and column normalization answer different conditional questions, while global normalization answers joint frequency questions. Always state which coordinate is conditioned on and which denominator is used.

Feature marginals alone cannot reveal all dependence between features. A generative model that replaces a joint distribution by the product of marginals makes an independence assumption; it needs justification beyond matching each feature histogram separately.
