## Intuition: a question and a scale

A written equation looks like a fact. It is really a question. When you read $3x+2=x+10$ you are asked: **for which numbers $x$ are the two sides equal?** For $x=4$ both sides are $14$. For $x=0$ they are $2$ and $10$. The unknown is a placeholder, and the equation picks out the numbers that fit.

A pair of kitchen scales is the right picture. The two pans hold the two sides. If they balance, the sides are equal, and *whatever you do to one pan you must do to the other* if you want them to keep balancing: take a kilogram off both, double both, halve both. The solving procedure is nothing else. Each step produces a new statement with the **same answers**, only simpler, until the unknown stands alone and the answer can be read off.

Two things can go wrong, and both are the subject of this chapter. Some steps do *not* keep the answers: multiplying by $0$ destroys them, and squaring or dividing by an expression that can be $0$ changes them. And the picture has to be extended to *inequalities*, where "the pan that is lower" is a statement about order and one operation, multiplying by a negative number, turns the order around.

This chapter prepares the next one, <Xref to="/chapters/functions/">Functions as input, transformation and output</Xref>, where the same letter $x$ stops being an unknown to be found and becomes an input that varies. It uses the squares and roots of <Xref to="/chapters/quantities/">the first chapter</Xref> and the number line of <Xref to="/chapters/coordinates/">the second</Xref>.

## Definition: expressions, equations, inequalities and their solutions

**Expression.** An expression is a recipe for a number, built from numbers, letters and the operations of arithmetic: $3x+2$, $x^{2}-1$, $\frac{y}{4}$. It has no "$=$" and is not true or false. It has a value once its letters have values.

**Variable, parameter, unknown.** A letter stands for a number, and the word used says what the letter is doing. An **unknown** is a letter whose value we are trying to find ($x$ in $3x+2=x+10$). A **variable** is a letter that is allowed to take many values, and we study what happens as it does ($x$ in $f(x)=3x+2$). A **parameter** is a letter that is fixed for a given problem but could have been another number ($a$ in $ax+b$). The same symbol can be any of them, depending on the question.

**Equation.** An equation states that two expressions have the same value: $L(x)=R(x)$. It is a **condition on $x$**. It is true for some values of $x$, false for others.

**Inequality.** Replace "$=$" by $<$, $\le$, $>$ or $\ge$. The statement is again a condition on $x$.

**Solution set.** The **solution set** of a statement is the set of all numbers $x$ that make it true. Two statements are **equivalent** when they have the same solution set. Solving means replacing a statement by equivalent ones until the answer is visible.

**Identity.** An equation that is true for *every* $x$, such as $2(x+1)=2x+2$, is an **identity**. An equation that is true for *no* $x$, such as $2(x+1)=2x+1$, is a **contradiction**. Everything else is a **conditional equation**: it is true for some $x$ and false for others.

**Balanced operation.** An operation applied to both sides of a statement is **balanced** when the new statement is equivalent to the old. The balanced operations for a linear statement $a_1x+b_1\ \square\ a_2x+b_2$ are:

- add the same number, or the same multiple of $x$, to both sides (subtracting is adding a negative number);
- multiply both sides by a number $k\neq 0$, or divide both sides by $k\neq 0$.

Each is **reversible**: adding $c$ is undone by adding $-c$, and multiplying by $k$ is undone by dividing by $k$. That is exactly why it preserves the solution set. If a step turns $S$ into $S'$ and the inverse step turns $S'$ back into $S$, then every solution of $S$ is a solution of $S'$ and vice versa. Multiplying by $0$ has no inverse, and the stage refuses it.

## Keeping the balance

A linear equation $a_1x+b_1=a_2x+b_2$ has a solution set of exactly one of three kinds. Move the $x$ terms to the left and the constants to the right, which are two balanced steps. What remains is
$$
(a_1-a_2)\,x=b_2-b_1 .
$$
Let $m=a_1-a_2$ and $c=b_2-b_1$. Then:

| value of $m$ | the equation $mx=c$ | solution set |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $m\neq 0$ | divide by $m$: $x=c/m$ | exactly one number |
| $m=0$, $c\neq 0$ | $0=c$, which is false | no solution |
| $m=0$, $c=0$ | $0=0$, which is true | every $x$ |

The stage follows this: the beam, the history and the inspector are all computed from the same statement. The beam shows $L(x)$ in the left pan and $R(x)$ in the right pan at the trial value of $x$. The pan with the larger value is lower, and the beam is **exactly level** only when the two sides are equal, that is, at a solution of an equation. The amount of tilt is schematic (it grows with the difference but is capped so that the picture stays on the page); the inspector shows the true numbers $L(x)$, $R(x)$ and $L(x)-R(x)$.

Look at the presets. For $2x+3=2x+7$ the $x$ terms cancel and the history ends with the false statement $0=4$; the solution set is empty, and the verdict is marked as a caution. For $2x+3=2x+3$ they cancel to the true $0=0$ and every $x$ works.

The history is a record. Selecting an earlier step with the stepper or the slider **shows** that statement; it never undoes anything, and the solution set stays the same at every step. You can remove the last step explicitly with its own button, which is a different action.

### Why multiplying by $0$ is refused

Start from $x=3$ and multiply both sides by $0$. The result is $0=0$, which is true for every $x$. The original had one solution and the new statement has all of them. Since no number turns $0$ back into $3$, the step cannot be undone. A step that changes the solution set is not balanced, and the stage shows its reason instead of accepting it. Dividing by $0$ is refused for the plain reason that division by $0$ is undefined.

## Inequalities and the order-reversing rule

An inequality such as $5x-1\ge 2x+8$ is solved by the same balanced steps, with its relation carried along. Adding a number to both sides keeps the relation. Multiplying by a **positive** $k$ keeps it. The one new fact is this.

> If $p<q$ and $k<0$, then $kp>kq$.

*Proof.* Since $p<q$, the number $q-p$ is positive. A negative number times a positive number is negative, so $k(q-p)<0$, that is $kq-kp<0$, that is $kq<kp$. $\square$

So **multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the relation**: $<$ becomes $>$, and $\le$ becomes $\ge$. Geometrically, multiplying by $-1$ reflects the number line through $0$, which swaps left and right, so what was to the left is now to the right; multiplying by $-3$ reflects and then stretches by $3$.

<Stage caption="Two numbers p and q on a number line, and their products with k below. Drag p and q or type them, and change k. For k greater than 0 the arrow keeps its direction; for k less than 0 it reverses. With k equal to 0 both products collapse to 0 and the step is rejected.">
  <ReversalStage client:load />
</Stage>

The stage states the **counterexample** in numbers. With $p=2$, $q=5$ and $k=-2$, the true order is $2<5$, and the products are $kp=-4$ and $kq=-10$. If you forgot to reverse and wrote $-4<-10$, you would have written a false statement. The true one is $-4>-10$. The multiplier $k=0$ is rejected for a different reason: $0\cdot p$ and $0\cdot q$ are both $0$, so the strict inequality $p<q$ becomes the false $0<0$, or loses its meaning altogether, and nothing can be divided back.

The solution set of an inequality is an **interval**, with an exact endpoint and an end that is open or closed. For $5x-1\ge 2x+8$ the steps give $3x\ge 9$, then $x\ge 3$: the set $[3,\infty)$, and $3$ belongs to it because the relation allows equality. For $4-2x<x+10$ the steps give $-3x<6$, then dividing by $-3$ (reversing) $x>-2$: the set $(-2,\infty)$, with $-2$ excluded. A square bracket or a filled dot means *included*; a round bracket or an open dot means *excluded*.

## Squaring is not a balanced step

Squaring both sides is tempting: it removes a square root. But the step $a=b\ \Rightarrow\ a^{2}=b^{2}$ only goes one way. The squared equation $a^{2}=b^{2}$ is also true when $a=-b$, so the new statement can have **more** solutions than the old. The extra ones are called **extraneous roots**.

Take the family $\sqrt{x+p}=x+q$ with whole numbers $p,q$. The square root sign means the **non-negative** root, so a solution must make the right side non-negative as well. Write $t=x+q$ for the right side. Then $x+p=t-q+p$, and squaring $x+p=(x+q)^{2}$ gives
$$
t^{2}=t+(p-q),\qquad t=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{D}}{2},\qquad D=4p-4q+1 .
$$
So $x=t-q=\frac{1-2q\pm\sqrt{D}}{2}$, which is the quadratic $x^{2}+(2q-1)x+(q^{2}-p)=0$ with discriminant $D$. The number $D$ is always odd.

A candidate $x_0$ solves the **original** equation exactly when its right side $x_0+q=t$ is **not negative**. If $t<0$, the number $x_0$ solves $-\sqrt{x+p}=x+q$ instead: the other sign of the root. Because the two values of $t$ add up to $1$, the larger one is at least $\tfrac12$, so **at least one candidate is always valid** whenever there are candidates. Whether the second is valid depends on the sign of its $t$.

<Stage caption="The curve y = √(x + p) (solid), the same curve with the opposite sign (dashed) and the line y = x + q. Each root of the squared equation sits on the line: a filled marker on the solid curve means it solves the original equation, an open-looking marker on the dashed curve means it solves the other sign and is extraneous. Change p and q, or use the presets.">
  <ExtraneousStage client:load />
</Stage>

The preset $p=2$, $q=0$ is $\sqrt{x+2}=x$. Squaring gives $x+2=x^{2}$, so $x^{2}-x-2=0$ and $(x-2)(x+1)=0$. The candidates are $2$ and $-1$. Substitute **into the original equation**: for $x=2$, $\sqrt{4}=2$ and the right side is $2$, so it is valid. For $x=-1$, $\sqrt{1}=1$ but the right side is $-1$: extraneous. It is a solution of $-\sqrt{x+2}=x$ instead.

The preset $p=1$, $q=0$ has the irrational candidates $\frac{1\pm\sqrt5}{2}$. The sign of $\frac{1-\sqrt5}{2}$ is decided *exactly* (it is negative because $\sqrt5>1$), not by looking at its decimal $-0.618034\ldots$. The stage also shows $p=0$, $q=1$ where $D=-3<0$: there is no real candidate and squaring cannot create any.

> The only reliable test of a candidate is to put it back into the **original** equation.

### The honest scope of this family

Squaring is one of several steps that can add roots. Multiplying both sides by an expression that can be $0$ does it too. This chapter treats one family exactly, so that the claim "a candidate is valid exactly when $x+q\ge0$" can be checked on every case. Other equations with radicals, and equations with fractions, need the same discipline: find the candidates by whatever steps you like, then check them in the original.

## Dividing by something that can be zero

The opposite danger is losing a root. Consider $x(x-2)=3(x-2)$. A tempting step is to divide both sides by $(x-2)$, which leaves $x=3$. But the division is allowed only where $x-2\neq0$, that is for $x\neq2$. At $x=2$ both sides equal $0$, so $x=2$ **is** a solution, and the division threw it away.

The safe way is to move everything to one side and factor:
$$
x(x-2)-3(x-2)=0\iff(x-2)(x-3)=0\iff x=2\ \text{or}\ x=3 .
$$
A product is $0$ exactly when one of its factors is $0$, so both roots appear. In general $x(x-c)=m(x-c)$ is equivalent to $(x-c)(x-m)=0$.

<Stage caption="The left side x(x − c) is a parabola and the right side m(x − c) is a line. They cross where the equation is true. The marker labelled lost is the solution that dividing by (x − c) throws away; when c equals m there is a single touching point and nothing is lost.">
  <FallacyStage client:load />
</Stage>

When $c=m$ the equation is $(x-c)^{2}=0$ with the single root $c$. Dividing gives $x=m=c$: nothing is lost *as a value*, but the division was still not justified. The stage keeps both cases apart.

The same lesson applies to simplifying a fraction such as $\frac{x^{2}-4}{x-2}=x+2$: the two sides agree for every $x\neq2$, but the left side is *undefined* at $2$. A simplification that is valid only where a divisor is not $0$ carries that restriction with it. The restriction on $x$ is called a **domain restriction**.

## Expanding brackets: identities and slips

The **distributive law** says that multiplication spreads over addition: $a(bx+c)=abx+ac$ for every $a,b,c,x$. It is an identity. Written as an equation $a(bx+c)=abx+ac$, its solution set is every $x$.

The most common slip multiplies only the first term: $a(bx+c)=abx+c$. The stage compares the two. Write the equation as a pair of lines $y=a(bx+c)$ and $y=abx+c$. They have the same slope $ab$, so they are parallel. They coincide when the constants agree, $ac=c$, which happens exactly when $c(a-1)=0$, that is $a=1$ or $c=0$. Otherwise they are a constant distance $c(a-1)$ apart, and the slip's equation is a **contradiction**: true for no $x$. A single numerical check at one $x$ already catches it.

<Stage caption="The bracket a(bx + c) as a solid line and its expansion as a dashed line. Pick whether the expansion multiplies every term or only the first. At the chosen x the inspector shows both values and their gap. The lines coincide for an identity and are parallel for the slip.">
  <ExpressionStage client:load />
</Stage>

This is the difference between the words in the definition. The bracket is an **expression**. Setting it equal to its correct expansion makes an **identity**, true for every $x$. Setting it equal to the slip makes a **contradiction**, true for no $x$ (except in the harmless cases $a=1$ or $c=0$, where the slip happens to be right). An equation like $2(x+1)=x+5$ is **conditional**: true only at $x=3$.

Reading $f(x)=ax+b$ is a first look at the next chapter: the same expression $ax+b$, but now $x$ is an input, and $a$ and $b$ are parameters that define the rule.

## Worked examples

Solving a linear equation is three moves in a fixed order, and it pays to see them as steps:

1. **Collect the $x$ terms** on one side by adding the opposite of the unwanted $x$ term to both sides.
2. **Collect the constants** on the other side the same way.
3. **Divide** by the number in front of $x$ (reversing an inequality if it is negative) and then **check** in the *original* statement.

Step through five cases. The first is $3x+2=x+10$. The second has a solution that is not a whole number. The third is the inequality $4-2x<x+10$, where the last step reverses the relation. The fourth is $\sqrt{x+2}=x$ with its extraneous root. The last is the division that loses a root.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Do the operation to one side only">
Adding $2$ to the left side alone changes the statement. The balance is kept only if the *same* operation is applied to **both** sides. The history in the stage writes the operation on each side before it simplifies, so you can see it was done to both.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Multiplying or dividing by a negative number keeps the inequality">
It reverses it. From $-3x<6$ the correct step is $x>-2$, not $x<-2$. Test one value: $x=-5$ makes $-3x<6$ false ($15<6$), and $x<-2$ is true there, so the two statements disagree. Subtracting a negative number does *not* reverse anything: only multiplying or dividing does.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Every root of the squared equation is a root of the original">
Squaring adds the roots of the equation with the other sign of the root. Always substitute the candidates **into the original**. In $\sqrt{x+2}=x$ the candidate $-1$ fails: $\sqrt{1}=1\neq-1$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Divide both sides by the common factor">
In $x(x-2)=3(x-2)$ the common factor $(x-2)$ is $0$ at $x=2$. Dividing by it discards the solution $x=2$. Move everything to one side and factor instead.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="a(bx + c) is abx + c">
The factor $a$ multiplies every term in the bracket: $a(bx+c)=abx+ac$. With the slip, the two sides are parallel lines that never meet when $a\neq1$ and $c\neq0$, so the "equation" is true for no $x$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="An equation with no solution is a wrong equation">
An equation that reduces to a false statement such as $0=4$ is correct *as a question*: its answer is the empty set. "No solution" is a legitimate answer, as legitimate as "$x=4$", and so is "every $x$" when it reduces to $0=0$.
</Callout>

## Numerical considerations

The mathematics above is exact. A program that solves equations with doubles is not, and two problems appear even for tiny, harmless-looking equations.

**Equality of the answer.** Solve $0.1x+0.2=0.3$ by hand: subtract $0.2$, divide by $0.1$, and the answer is exactly $1$. In doubles, `(0.3 - 0.2) / 0.1` is `0.9999999999999998`, and `== 1` is `False`. The table solves several equations exactly with the model's rational arithmetic, next to the double a program holds. A row marked "no" is a case where the program's answer is a different number from the true one.

<NumericsTable />

<Callout kind="numerical">
Do not test the answer of an equation with `==` on floats. Either keep the coefficients exact (`Fraction` built from strings, never from floats), or compare with a tolerance (`math.isclose`) chosen for the problem. The check "substitute and compare the two sides" has the same weakness.
</Callout>

**A coefficient that should vanish.** The three-way answer depends on whether $m=a_1-a_2$ is exactly $0$. In doubles, `(0.1 + 0.2) - 0.3` is not $0$ but $2^{-54}\approx5.55\cdot10^{-17}$. The equation $(0.1+0.2)x+1=0.3x+2$ has **no solution** (the $x$ terms cancel to $0$ and leave $0=1$), but computed in floats the program divides $1$ by $2^{-54}$ and reports $x\approx1.8\cdot10^{16}$ with full confidence. The reference file above shows both calculations: the float one and the exact one that returns `none`.

**Substitution with a tolerance.** The extraneous-root test in the reference file compares `math.sqrt(x + p)` with `x + q` using `math.isclose`. That is adequate for the whole-number parameters of this family, where a valid root has a right side of at least $0$ and an invalid one has a negative right side of at least about $0.6$ in size. For other equations the tolerance decides the verdict, and a tolerance that is too wide accepts a false root while one that is too narrow rejects a true one. The exact test (a `Fraction` whose square root is itself a `Fraction`) cannot be fooled but works only when the root is rational.

## Connections to engineering and machine learning

- **Undoing a transformation.** Standardising a feature, $z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$, is an affine map, and getting back to the original scale is *solving that equation for $x$*: multiply by $\sigma$ (not $0$) and add $\mu$. A feature with $\sigma=0$ is the "multiply by $0$" case in disguise: the map cannot be undone, and a constant column cannot be standardised.
- **Learning rates are factors in an inequality.** Gradient descent on $f(w)=(w-c)^{2}$ updates $w\leftarrow w-\eta\cdot2(w-c)$, so the distance to the minimiser is multiplied by $1-2\eta$ at every step. The distance shrinks when $\lvert1-2\eta\rvert<1$. Written as $-1<1-2\eta<1$ and solved with the steps of this chapter, subtracting $1$ gives $-2<-2\eta<0$, and dividing by $-2$ **reverses both inequalities**: $0<\eta<1$. The condition $\eta>0$ matters: a negative rate moves away from the minimum. The reference file runs three steps for several rates.
- **One-dimensional least squares.** Fitting a single weight to $xw=y$ gives $w=y/x$, valid only for $x\neq0$. When $x=0$ the equation says $0=y$: no solution if $y\neq0$, every $w$ if $y=0$. The three cases of this chapter are the three behaviours of the linear system in one unknown, and they return in higher dimensions.
- **Thresholds on a loss.** A stopping rule such as "stop when the loss is below $\varepsilon$" is an inequality in the parameters. Rearranging it by hand needs the same reversal rule whenever a negative coefficient is involved.
- **Domain guards in code.** The division fallacy is the source of many runtime errors: a formula simplified by hand is fine for $x\neq2$ and a `ZeroDivisionError` at $2$. Write the restriction down with the formula.
- **Looking ahead.** In <Xref to="/chapters/functions/">Functions</Xref> the expression $ax+b$ becomes a rule with a domain, and an equation $f(x)=y$ asks for the inputs that produce a given output.

## Summary and checkpoints

- An equation or inequality is a **condition** on an unknown; its **solution set** is the set of numbers that satisfy it. A linear equation has exactly one solution, none, or every number.
- A **balanced** step (add the same thing to both sides, multiply or divide both sides by a non-zero number) is reversible, so it keeps the solution set. Multiplying by $0$ is not reversible and is refused.
- Multiplying or dividing by a **negative** number reverses an inequality, because it reflects the line through $0$. Test a value to catch a missed reversal.
- Squaring is **not** a balanced step: it can add roots (extraneous roots). Substitute each candidate into the **original** equation.
- Dividing by an expression that can be $0$ can lose a root. Move everything to one side and factor instead.
- $a(bx+c)=abx+ac$ is an identity; the slip $abx+c$ gives a contradiction unless $a=1$ or $c=0$.
- In code: keep coefficients exact, never compare a float answer with `==`, and test whether a coefficient that should cancel is exactly $0$.
