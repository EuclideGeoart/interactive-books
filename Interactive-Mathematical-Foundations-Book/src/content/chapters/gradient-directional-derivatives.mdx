## Intuition: one vector answers every direction

Walking from a point in a direction $\mathbf u$, the height changes at some rate. You might expect a different calculation for every direction. For a differentiable function there is a single vector — the gradient — and the rate in direction $\mathbf u$ is just its component along $\mathbf u$. The profile plot shows the consequence: as the direction turns, the rate traces a cosine wave.

## Definition

The **gradient** of $f$ at $p$ is the vector of partial derivatives,

$$
\nabla f(p)=\begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial x\\ \partial f/\partial y\end{bmatrix}.
$$

For a **unit** vector $\mathbf u$, the **directional derivative** is

$$
D_{\mathbf u}f(p)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(p+h\mathbf u)-f(p)}{h}.
$$

When $f$ is differentiable, $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$. A nonzero direction vector $\mathbf d$ is normalised first: $\mathbf u=\mathbf d/\|\mathbf d\|$. The zero vector has no direction.

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

The gradient is exact at rational points. The unnormalised product $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf d$ is exact; dividing by $\|\mathbf d\|$ is exact when $\|\mathbf d\|^2$ is the square of a rational and is otherwise shown with $\approx$. The gradient arrow is drawn at the scale stated in the title, so its direction is faithful and its length is proportional. The level-curve tangent is perpendicular to the gradient, and the inspector checks $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf t=0$ exactly.

## Derivation: why the dot product appears

Differentiability means $f(p+\boldsymbol\delta)=f(p)+\nabla f\cdot\boldsymbol\delta+o(\|\boldsymbol\delta\|)$. Put $\boldsymbol\delta=h\mathbf u$ and divide by $h$:

$$
\frac{f(p+h\mathbf u)-f(p)}{h}=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u+o(1).
$$

Letting $h\to0$ gives $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf u$ and $\nabla f$. The rate is largest when $\theta=0$ (steepest ascent, value $\|\nabla f\|$), smallest when $\theta=\pi$, and zero when $\mathbf u\perp\nabla f$. Along a level curve $f$ is constant, so its tangent direction has zero rate: the gradient is perpendicular to regular level curves.

## Worked example

For $f=x^2/2+2y^2$ at $(1,\tfrac12)$: $\nabla f=(x,4y)=(1,2)$. With $\mathbf d=(3,4)$, $\|\mathbf d\|=5$, so

$$
D_{\mathbf u}f=\frac{(1,2)\cdot(3,4)}{5}=\frac{11}{5}.
$$

The steepest rate is $\sqrt{1+4}=\sqrt5$, and the tangent to the level curve is $\mathbf t=(-2,1)$, for which $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf t=0$.

## Stationary points and undefined cases

Where $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$ (the **Stationary point** preset), every directional derivative is $0$ and there is no direction of steepest ascent; the stage draws no gradient arrow rather than an arbitrary one. A zero direction vector cannot be normalised, so the directional derivative is reported as undefined. At points where $f$ is not differentiable, the formula $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$ is not guaranteed even when the partials exist; the surfaces here are polynomials, which are differentiable everywhere.

## Common mistakes

Using an unnormalised direction, which scales the "rate" by $\|\mathbf d\|$. Drawing the gradient tangent to the contour instead of perpendicular to it. Assuming the gradient points toward a minimum: it points toward the steepest *increase*; the steepest decrease is $-\nabla f$.

## Numerical considerations

Normalising a tiny direction vector divides by a tiny number and amplifies rounding; the zero vector must be rejected explicitly, not left to produce NaN. When $\nabla f$ is nearly zero its direction is numerically unreliable even if its components are accurate: steepest-ascent directions near stationary points are ill-conditioned.

## Engineering and ML connections

Gradient descent (VI.4) steps along $-\nabla f$ because it is locally the steepest decrease. Directional derivatives appear in line searches and in Jacobian–vector products, which automatic differentiation (III.10) computes without forming the whole gradient. Level-set geometry also underlies constraints in optimisation (VI.9).

## Summary and checkpoints

The gradient collects the partial derivatives; for a unit direction $\mathbf u$, $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$. It points along steepest increase with rate $\|\nabla f\|$ and is perpendicular to regular level curves. At stationary points it vanishes, and zero directions have no rate.
