## Intuition: possibilities are not observations

A die in your hand has not yet produced a result. A probability model describes what may happen and assigns weights to those possibilities. After a roll, you observe one face. The five other faces remain possibilities for a new roll under the same model; the observation does not erase them from the sample space.

An event answers a question such as “was the face even?” It collects every outcome that would make the answer yes. A probability is the total mass of that collection, not a promise about the next observed result.

## Definition

A finite **sample space** $\Omega$ is a nonempty set of distinct outcomes. A probability model assigns a mass $p_\omega$ to each outcome, subject to

$$
p_\omega\ge0,\qquad \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p_\omega=1.
$$

An **event** $A$ is a subset of $\Omega$, with probability $P(A)=\sum_{\omega\in A}p_\omega$. The empty event has probability zero and the whole sample space has probability one. A possible label may have mass zero in a specified model; nonempty does not always mean positive probability.

For the canonical **fair six-sided die**, $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ and every mass is $1/6$. Fairness is an explicit assumption, not a consequence of having six labels.

## Derivation: when counting is enough

If there are $n$ equally likely outcomes, normalization gives $np=1$, hence $p=1/n$. If $A$ contains $m$ of them, then

$$
P(A)=m\frac1n=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}.
$$

This shortcut follows from equal masses. With unequal masses, the set still tells you which terms to add, but its size no longer tells you their sum. Nor does a short observed sequence determine the weights of a future experiment.

## Worked examples

For $A=\{2,4,6\}$ on the fair die, three selected outcomes each contribute $1/6$, so $P(A)=1/2$. For $B=\{4,5,6\}$, $P(B)=1/2$ as well. Their shared faces are $4$ and $6$, with total mass $1/3$; equal event masses do not make the events identical.

If we describe only the number of heads, the labels $0,1,2$ have masses $1/4,1/2,1/4$. Grouping outcomes changes the labels, not the total mass associated with each group.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Three even observations in a row do not imply that the model now contains only even faces. A fair independent die does not owe you an odd face after a streak. Conversely, a streak does not prove a die fair: fairness is stipulated here; estimating a physical die's probabilities from data is a later statistical problem.

For a counterexample to counting alone, assign red, green, and blue masses $1/2,1/3,1/6$. Selecting green and blue gives $1/2$, although two of three labels are selected. Treating those labels as equally likely silently replaces the model.

## Numerical considerations

The finite examples have exact rational answers. Python constructs `Fraction(1, 6)` from integers; constructing a fraction from a binary floating-point approximation would preserve that approximation instead. NumPy arrays use floating-point masses and sums, so the reference marks these comparisons as approximate and checks absolute error with an explicit $10^{-12}$ tolerance, not a claim of exact equality.

An empirical frequency is an integer count divided by the number of observed trials. It may differ substantially from model probability at small sample sizes. The seeded sequence is reproducible in the reference environment, bounded, and illustrative; it is not a proof of a probability or a cross-language random-generator contract.

## Engineering and ML connections

Finite models appear in categorical predictions, randomized tests, and simulation design. Before using a probability vector, check that labels are distinct, weights are nonnegative, and total mass is one. A model's predicted probability and an observed class count answer different questions; mixing them can make evaluation circular.
