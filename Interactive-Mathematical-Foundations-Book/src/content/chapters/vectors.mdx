## Intuition: a vector is a move

Walk three blocks east and two blocks north. That instruction is a **displacement**: it says how far to move, not where you are. It means the same thing from your front door and from the library; only the place you end up changes.

A vector in the plane is exactly such a move, written as two numbers, a column:

$$
\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}
$$

The first number is the movement along $x$ (right is positive) and the second is the movement along $y$ (up is positive). We draw it as an arrow, but the arrow is a picture of the move; the two numbers are the move.

## Points and displacements

A **point** is a place: $P=(1,-1)$ names a location. A **vector** is a move. They are written with the same two numbers, which is the source of a lot of confusion, so it is worth separating them carefully.

The two are tied by two equations: applying a move to a point gives another point, and two points give the move between them.

$$
Q=P+\mathbf v,\qquad \mathbf v=Q-P .
$$

Slide the arrow around and watch the inspector. The points $P$ and $Q$ change; $\mathbf v$, its length and its direction do not. Now move only the tip: the displacement changes, because it is *defined* as the difference of the two points. An arrow drawn starting at the origin is the special case $P=(0,0)$, called standard position; it is the one place where the point $Q$ and the vector $\mathbf v$ happen to have the same numbers.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="The vector is not its starting point">
An arrow starting at $(1,-1)$ and an arrow starting at $(-2,4)$ are the *same vector* if they have the same components. Where the arrow is drawn is a choice about the picture; what it stores is only the move.
</Callout>

## Definition

A **vector in the plane** is an ordered pair of real numbers, written as a column,

$$
\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix},
$$

where $x$ is the **horizontal component** and $y$ is the **vertical component**. Two vectors are equal exactly when both components are equal. The **zero vector** $\mathbf 0=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}$ is the move that goes nowhere.

Throughout this book the plane has $+x$ to the right and $+y$ **up**, vectors are columns, and angles are in radians measured from the $+x$ axis, **counter-clockwise turns positive and clockwise turns negative** (so $(1,-1)$ has direction $-\pi/4$). A vector has two derived quantities: its **magnitude** $\lVert\mathbf v\rVert$ (how long the move is) and, when it is not the zero vector, its **direction** $\theta$ (which way).

## Magnitude: Pythagoras on the components

The move $\mathbf v=(x,y)$ goes $x$ across and $y$ up. Those two legs meet at a right angle, so the straight-line length of the arrow is the hypotenuse:

$$
\lVert\mathbf v\rVert=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}} .
$$

Pythagoras' theorem says the *areas* of the squares on the legs add up to the area of the square on the hypotenuse. Here that is literal: drag the vector and compare the three squares.

<Stage caption="The square on the x-leg has area x², the square on the y-leg has area y², and the square on the arrow has area ‖v‖². The two small areas always add to the large one.">
  <PythagorasStage client:load />
</Stage>

Notice what the inspector says for $(3,2)$: the square on the arrow has area $13$, exactly, so the side is $\sqrt{13}$ exactly, and $\sqrt{13}\approx 3.605551$ is only a *rounding*. For $(3,4)$ the area is $25$ and the length is exactly $5$. The book writes $=$ for the first kind of statement and $\approx$ for the second, and never mixes them.

Two properties follow at once from the formula. The length is never negative, and it is $0$ only for the zero vector, because a sum of squares is $0$ only when both squares are. And $\lVert -\mathbf v\rVert=\lVert\mathbf v\rVert$, since squaring erases signs: reversing a move does not change how long it is.

## Direction: the angle from the x-axis

The direction of a nonzero vector is the signed angle $\theta$ from the positive $x$ axis to the arrow: counter-clockwise is positive, clockwise is negative. In the right triangle, $\tan\theta$ is rise over run, so for a vector pointing into the right half-plane

$$
\theta=\arctan\frac{y}{x}\qquad (x>0).
$$

That is a perfectly good formula, for a vector pointing right. The trouble is everything else.

## Why $\arctan(y/x)$ is not enough

Take $\mathbf v=(-3,2)$, which points up and to the left. Then $y/x=-2/3$ and $\arctan(-2/3)\approx-0.588$ radians, which is a direction pointing **down and to the right**: exactly backwards. The vector $-\mathbf v=(3,-2)$ has the identical ratio $-2/3$, so a formula that sees only the ratio *cannot tell $\mathbf v$ from $-\mathbf v$*. And $\arctan$ is only able to answer between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$, the right half-plane, so for a vector in the left half-plane it is wrong by exactly half a turn.

<Stage caption="Jump between quadrants and axes. The dashed line through the origin carries v and −v, which share the ratio y/x whenever x is not 0 (on the y-axis no ratio exists, although the direction does); the red arrow is where arctan(y/x) points when it disagrees with the true direction.">
  <DirectionStage client:load />
</Stage>

The function that fixes this is $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$. It takes the two components separately, so it can see their signs, and returns the angle in $(-\pi,\pi]$. For $x>0$ it equals $\arctan(y/x)$. For $x<0$ it equals $\arctan(y/x)+\pi$ when $y\ge0$ and $\arctan(y/x)-\pi$ when $y<0$. When $x=0$ a vertical vector has a perfectly valid direction but no defined ratio $y/x$; $\operatorname{atan2}$ does not divide, so $\operatorname{atan2}(y,0)=\pi/2$ for $y>0$ and $-\pi/2$ for $y<0$.

$$
\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x),\qquad \operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\arctan\frac{y}{x}\in\{0,\ \pi,\ -\pi\}\quad(x\neq0).
$$

This identity needs $x\neq0$, because $y/x$ must exist. The correction is *exactly* $0$, $\pi$ or $-\pi$, decided by the signs of $x$ and $y$ alone. Here is the whole picture in one table, every cell computed by the same model the stage above uses.

<ProbeTable />

And there is one vector for which **neither** formula has a right answer: the zero vector. It has no direction, because it points nowhere. The stage shows this as an *undefined* state, not as the number $0$.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Adding the components to get the length">
$\lVert(5,12)\rVert$ is $13$, not $17$. Components are the legs of a right triangle; lengths add only along a straight line.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Using arctan(y/x) as the direction">
It is right only when $x>0$. For $x<0$ it is half a turn off, and for $x=0$ it divides by zero. Always use $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="The zero vector points along the x-axis">
`math.atan2(0.0, 0.0)` returns `0.0` without complaint. That is a convention, not a direction: the zero vector has none. Check for it explicitly.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="misconception" title="A vector lives where it is drawn">
Sliding an arrow changes the points $P$ and $Q$, never the vector $\mathbf v=Q-P$.
</Callout>

## Numerical considerations

Mathematically $\lVert\mathbf v\rVert=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. In floating point, squaring first can lose the answer completely: $(10^{-200})^2=10^{-400}$ is smaller than the smallest double, and $(10^{200})^2$ is larger than the largest. The library function `hypot` avoids both by rescaling before it squares.

<NumericsTable />

<Callout kind="numerical">
Use `math.hypot` or `np.hypot`, not `sqrt(x*x + y*y)`. Note that `np.linalg.norm` squares first, so it can return `0.0` for a tiny nonzero vector. Compare lengths with a tolerance rather than `==`, and keep exact values (such as $\sqrt{13}$) as exact values until you must print them.
</Callout>

Two more points. Negative zero exists: $-0.0$ equals $0.0$ in comparisons but `atan2(0.0, -0.0)` returns $\pi$ while `atan2(0.0, 0.0)` returns $0$. The stages here normalise $-0$ to $0$ so the displayed direction is stable. And the angle from `atan2` jumps from $\pi$ to $-\pi$ across the negative $x$ axis; that is a feature of the *range*, not a change of direction.

## Connections to engineering and machine learning

- **Features as vectors.** A data point with two measurements is a vector; its length is its distance from the origin, the Euclidean norm that appears everywhere from nearest-neighbour search to regularisation.
- **Normalisation.** Dividing a vector by its length gives a unit vector that keeps the direction and discards the scale; cosine similarity compares directions only. The zero vector has no direction, so normalising it needs a guard (the usual `eps` in code).
- **Robotics and graphics.** Headings, velocities and forces are vectors; `atan2` is how a robot turns a displacement to a target into a heading, and why it is the standard function in every such library.
- **Gradients.** The gradient of a function is a vector; its length is the steepness and its direction is the way uphill.

## Summary and checkpoints

- A vector in the plane is a column of two numbers, a displacement: *how far*, not *where*. Points are places; $Q=P+\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf v=Q-P$.
- Its magnitude is $\lVert\mathbf v\rVert=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, Pythagoras on the components. It is exact when the sum is a perfect square or a known radical, and otherwise a rounding.
- Its direction is $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$, not $\arctan(y/x)$, which is wrong by $\pm\pi$ in the left half-plane and does not exist on the $y$-axis.
- The zero vector has length $0$ and **no direction**.
- In code: `hypot` for length, `atan2` for direction, an explicit guard for zero.
