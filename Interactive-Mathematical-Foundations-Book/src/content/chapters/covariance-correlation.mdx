## Intuition: signs of centered products

When both coordinates lie above their means, their deviations have the same sign and their product is positive. The same is true when both lie below their means. Opposite deviations contribute negative products. Covariance adds those products using probability weights.

The picture must preserve mass: a point of probability $1/4$ contributes twice the weight of a point of probability $1/8$. A scatterplot's occupied locations alone are insufficient. Positive covariance suggests a shared linear direction; cancellation can hide a curved relationship.

## Definition

For finite second moments, write $\mu_X=\mathbb E[X]$ and $\mu_Y=\mathbb E[Y]$. Covariance is

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]
=\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}(x_i-\mu_X)(y_j-\mu_Y).
$$

If both variances are positive, correlation is

$$
\rho(X,Y)=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y},
\qquad -1\leq\rho\leq1.
$$

Covariance has the product of the two variables' units. Correlation is dimensionless. If either variable is constant on positive-mass outcomes, its variance is zero and correlation is undefined, even though covariance is well defined and zero.

## Mechanics: distinguish latent cells from mapped points

The latent table uses $U,V\in\{-1,0,1\}$ and canonical weights $[1,1,0;1,2,1;0,1,1]$. The mapped variables are $X=aU+b$ and $Y=cV+d$. The stage keeps the table clearly labeled by latent $U,V$, while its scatterplot and association rows describe mapped $X,Y$.

Change a scale or shift through the controls. If multiple latent cells map to one scatter location, their probability masses are merged. A zero scale is valid and collapses one mapped variable to a constant; it does not erase or rewrite the latent table. Reset restores identity maps and canonical weights.

## Derivation: centering removes shifts

Expanding the centered product gives

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb E[XY]-\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y].
$$

For affine transformations, $aU+b-\mathbb E[aU+b]=a(U-\mathbb E[U])$, hence

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(aU+b,cV+d)=ac\operatorname{Cov}(U,V).
$$

The two variances multiply by $a^2$ and $c^2$. When both scales are nonzero,

$$
\rho(aU+b,cV+d)=\frac{ac}{|a||c|}\rho(U,V).
$$

Shifts leave covariance and correlation unchanged. Positive changes of units preserve correlation. Reflecting exactly one axis flips its sign; reflecting both restores it. The correlation formula above does not extend to a zero scale.

## Worked examples

Canonical means are zero and both variances are $1/2$. Only the two mass-$1/8$ diagonal corners contribute nonzero $UV$, so

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(U,V)=\frac18+\frac18=\frac14,
\qquad \rho(U,V)=\frac{1/4}{\sqrt{(1/2)(1/2)}}=\frac12.
$$

For $X=-2U+3$ and $Y=3V-1$, means become $3,-1$, variances become $2,9/2$, and covariance becomes $-3/2$. Its correlation is $-1/2$.

A different table, $[0,0,1;0,1,0;0,0,1]$, assigns equal mass to $(-1,1),(0,0),(1,1)$. Here $V=U^2$. Its moments are

$$
\mathbb E[U]=0,\quad\mathbb E[V]=\frac23,\quad
\operatorname{Var}(U)=\frac23,\quad\operatorname{Var}(V)=\frac29.
$$

Symmetry gives $\mathbb E[UV]=0$, so covariance and correlation are zero. Yet $\mathbb P(V=0\mid U=0)=1$ differs from $\mathbb P(V=0)=1/3$. The dependence is nonlinear and exact.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Independence with finite second moments implies zero covariance: independence factors $\mathbb E[XY]$. The converse fails in the $V=U^2$ example. Zero correlation means no centered linear association; it does not mean no relationship.

Variance of a sum includes association:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)
+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y).
$$

For canonical $U,V$, the result is $3/2$, not $1$. Correlation also cannot establish causation: a common cause, selection rule, or measurement process can produce association without a direct causal effect.

## Numerical considerations

The stage preserves exact fractions for bounded weights, moments, covariance, and affine parameters. Correlation may require an irrational square root and is then labeled approximately. An exact zero variance produces an explicit undefined result, never a fabricated correlation of zero.

Centered products help avoid subtracting large nearly equal floating moments. Near-zero variance can still make a correlation numerically sensitive; displayed rounding must not decide whether the denominator is zero. For floating cross-checks, record the tolerance and keep theoretical weighted moments separate from sample estimators.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="A constant has undefined correlation">
Its covariance is zero because every centered deviation is zero. Its correlation asks for division by a zero standard deviation. Independence of the constant variable remains a separate, well-defined statement.
</Callout>

## Engineering and ML connections

Correlation can detect redundant linear feature directions and covariance can describe the spread of paired quantities. Changing measurement units rescales covariance, while positive unit conversion leaves correlation fixed. A zero-variance feature needs an explicit standardization policy.

Feature screening by correlation alone can discard a predictive nonlinear relationship. Observational associations also need a causal model or experimental design before they justify an intervention; a signed coefficient is not that justification.
