## Intuition: downhill direction plus a chosen distance

The gradient points toward the steepest local increase per unit Euclidean distance. Negating it gives a locally downhill direction. The learning rate scales that vector into a displacement, and adding the displacement produces a new parameter choice.

One step is not an instruction to move directly to the minimum. It uses information at the current point, and the shape can change along the way. Even a downhill direction can cross a valley and end at a higher loss if the distance is too large. The next step must evaluate the gradient again at the new point rather than reuse an earlier one.

## Definition: the update recurrence

For a differentiable objective, gradient descent with positive learning rate $\eta$ uses

$$
g_k=\nabla L(\theta_k),\qquad
\Delta\theta_k=-\eta g_k,\qquad
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+\Delta\theta_k.
$$

The gradient, update vector, and next parameter vector are distinct. $g_k$ contains sensitivity; $\Delta\theta_k$ contains a signed movement; $\theta_{k+1}$ is an absolute point. With a fixed objective and fixed rate, the recurrence is deterministic. A stopping policy decides when the finite computation ends, which is separate from the mathematical definition of an iteration.

The recurring model is $L=a(x-c)^2/2+2y^2$, with default $a=1,c=0$, gradient $(a(x-c),4y)$, and Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(a,4)$. This chapter uses full objective gradients; stochastic estimates and momentum are later algorithms with additional state or assumptions.

Every derivative for a step is evaluated at the same current vector before either coordinate changes. Computing the x update, then using that newly changed x to evaluate the y update would generally be a different algorithm. This separable quadratic can conceal such an error, so keep the simultaneous vector rule explicit.

## Derivation: why the negative gradient, and why it can overshoot

For a unit direction $u$, differentiability gives the first-order rate

$$
D_uL(\theta)=g\cdot u\geq-\norm{g},
$$

by Cauchy–Schwarz. For $g\ne0$, equality holds at $u=-g/\norm{g}$: the negative-gradient direction is steepest decrease under the Euclidean distance convention. Choosing displacement $-\eta g$ gives step length $\eta\norm{g}$ and linear predicted change $-\eta\norm{g}^2$. At $g=0$, the update is the well-defined zero vector; no normalization is needed.

The quadratic's exact expansion includes curvature:

$$
L(\theta-\eta g)-L(\theta)
=-\eta\norm{g}^2+\frac{\eta^2}{2}g^{\mathsf T}Hg.
$$

The first term is negative for a nonzero gradient, but the second term can dominate for a large rate. For the default Hessian $\operatorname{diag}(1,4)$,

$$
g^{\mathsf T}Hg\leq4\norm{g}^2,
\qquad L(\theta-\eta g)-L(\theta)
\leq-\eta(1-2\eta)\norm{g}^2.
$$

Thus $0<\eta<1/2$ guarantees a strict loss decrease whenever the gradient is nonzero for this particular quadratic. The largest curvature matters. Detailed convergence behavior belongs to VI.5; a small learning rate is not a universal guarantee for an arbitrary objective or invalid domain.

## Worked example: the complete canonical step

Substitute the current parameters before moving them:

$$
\theta_0=(2,1),\qquad
L_0=\frac{2^2}{2}+2(1)^2=4,\qquad g_0=(2,4).
$$

Scale both components and add the displacement:

$$
\Delta\theta_0=-\frac14(2,4)=\left(-\frac12,-1\right),
\qquad \theta_1=(2,1)+\left(-\frac12,-1\right)=\left(\frac32,0\right).
$$

The new loss is $L_1=(3/2)^2/2=9/8$. As an independent check, $\norm{g_0}^2=20$ and $g_0^{\mathsf T}Hg_0=68$. The exact loss change is $-20/4+68/32=-23/8$, agreeing with $9/8-4$.

For the second step, recompute $g_1=(3/2,0)$. The new displacement is $(-3/8,0)$, so $\theta_2=(9/8,0)$ and $L_2=81/128$. These are exact rational values; their dyadic denominators also make these first binary64 evaluations exactly representable.

More generally the component recurrence is

$$
x_{k+1}-c=(1-\eta a)(x_k-c),\qquad
y_{k+1}=(1-4\eta)y_k.
$$

For the default canonical run, y becomes zero after the first step, while $x_k=2(3/4)^k$. In exact arithmetic x tends to zero but remains positive at every finite k. A plotted line reaching the origin after rounding does not change that statement.

## Explore: predict an update before replaying it

Return to $(2,1)$ but change the rate to $3/4$. The gradient remains $(2,4)$ because the current point is unchanged. Predict displacement $(-3/2,-3)$ and next point $(1/2,-2)$. Its loss is $1/8+8=65/8$, which exceeds $4$. The direction is locally downhill, but the step overshoots in the high-curvature y direction.

Select the saddle $x^2-y^2$ at $(0,0)$. Its gradient is zero, so the run has no nonzero step. Compare nearby $(0,h)$: the loss is $-h^2$, proving that the stopped point is not a minimum. Repeat with the peak and the flat minimum to separate algorithmic stopping from classification.

Choose the absolute-value objective at its kink, then the logarithmic objective at $x=0$. The first has a valid loss but no classical gradient; the second has no valid loss at all. Neither permits an ordinary gradient-descent step from that state. Move the logarithmic start inside $x>0$ before evaluating an update, and verify the new point remains inside the domain as well.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Adding $+\eta g$ follows the ascent direction to first order. Replacing $\theta$ with $-\eta g$ confuses a displacement with a point. Reusing the old gradient after moving fails to implement the stated recurrence. In the canonical example the y component becomes zero, so the second gradient's y component must also be zero.

A falling loss over several steps does not certify a global optimum. The cubic can settle at its nonglobal local minimum; a saddle can stop immediately. Also keep derivatives with respect to parameters separate from derivatives with respect to data. Training updates model coefficients, while the observed input data are held fixed for this full-objective calculation.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Direction and finite-step outcome are different claims">
The negative gradient gives steepest local decrease for a fixed Euclidean step length. The rate determines a finite movement. Curvature and domain validity decide whether the actual endpoint improves the loss.
</Callout>

## Numerical considerations

The replay is an evaluated binary64 trajectory and is labeled with approximation signs. The exact `Fraction` demonstration is a separate mathematical calculation; do not use it to imply that a long browser run remains exact. With a non-dyadic rate, rounding starts immediately. With dyadic rates, eventually long products, small changes, or underflow can still affect the stored path.

A displacement smaller than the spacing of representable numbers near a coordinate can disappear when added. A repeated stored state then reports numerical stagnation, not a theorem that the real recurrence attained its limit. Large iterates or gradients can instead overflow. The NumPy reference rejects non-finite results, and the bounded explorer reports invalid domains or its stopping boundary instead of fabricating a valid endpoint.

Loss change, gradient norm, parameter scale, and iteration budget answer different diagnostic questions. This chapter exposes the update; VI.10 will examine stopping criteria in depth. Display precision must never determine whether the gradient is mathematically zero or whether an objective has a global minimum.

## Engineering and ML connections

For squared-error regression, each residual contributes a parameter derivative, and the full gradient aggregates these contributions. <Xref to="chapters/automatic-differentiation/">Automatic differentiation</Xref> obtains such gradients through a computational graph; the optimizer then performs the separate subtraction shown here. Differentiation computes sensitivity, while optimization uses it to choose the next parameters.

The <Xref to="chapters/gradient-directional-derivatives/">gradient chapter</Xref> supplies the directional argument, <Xref to="chapters/hessian-curvature/">curvature</Xref> supplies the finite-step correction, and <Xref to="chapters/convexity/">convexity</Xref> supplies some global guarantees. Neural-network training repeats the same vector update mechanism over many parameters, but its objective need not be convex. Coordinate units and objective scaling also affect the effective step, which motivates careful learning-rate choices rather than one universal setting.
