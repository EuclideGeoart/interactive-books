## Intuition: alignment with size

Two equally directed vectors reinforce the same move. Perpendicular vectors share no component along each other's direction. Opposite vectors disagree. The dot product encodes this with a positive value, zero, or a negative value. It also depends on length: doubling either vector doubles the product, even if the angle stays unchanged.

The parallel arrow in the stage shows the part of $\mathbf v$ along the line of $\mathbf u$. Its signed scalar component is positive on $\mathbf u$'s ray and negative on the opposite ray. Multiplying that scalar component by $\norm{\mathbf u}$ gives the dot product. The complete decomposition is the subject of <Xref to="chapters/vector-projection/">II.5 Projection</Xref>.

## Definition

For $\mathbf u,\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^2$,

$$
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y.
$$

The output is a **scalar**, not a vector. If both vectors are nonzero and $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ is their smaller, unsigned angle,

$$
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\norm{\mathbf u}\norm{\mathbf v}\cos\theta.
$$

Angles are in radians. The two vectors are **orthogonal** when their dot product is zero. For nonzero vectors this means perpendicular directions and $\theta=\pi/2$. The zero vector is algebraically orthogonal to every vector, but it has no direction and forms no defined angle.

## Mechanics: products before the sum

The inspector separates $u_xv_x$ and $u_yv_y$. Either product may be negative; their sum determines alignment. In the perpendicular preset $\mathbf u=(3,0)$ and $\mathbf v=(0,3)$, both products vanish. For $(2,1)$ and $(-1,2)$, they instead cancel as $-2+2=0$. Perpendicularity does not require axis alignment or a zero component.

For nonzero vectors, a positive product means an angle below $\pi/2$; a negative product means an angle above $\pi/2$. At identical directions $\theta=0$, and at opposite directions $\theta=\pi$. The arc always represents this smaller angle, rather than a directed full rotation. Turning clockwise and counter-clockwise to the same endpoint can therefore give the same angle.

## Derivation: components meet geometry

Form the triangle with sides $\mathbf u$, $\mathbf v$, and the vector between their tips. Expand the squared length of that last side:

$$
\norm{\mathbf u-\mathbf v}^2
=\norm{\mathbf u}^2+\norm{\mathbf v}^2
-2(u_xv_x+u_yv_y).
$$

The cosine rule for the same triangle gives

$$
\norm{\mathbf u-\mathbf v}^2
=\norm{\mathbf u}^2+\norm{\mathbf v}^2
-2\norm{\mathbf u}\norm{\mathbf v}\cos\theta.
$$

Equating the final terms proves the geometric identity. Symmetry follows directly from multiplication of real numbers: $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u$. Distributivity gives $\mathbf u\cdot(a\mathbf v+b\mathbf w)=a(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)+b(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w)$. Finally, $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v=\norm{\mathbf v}^2$.

The geometric identity also implies $|\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v|\le\norm{\mathbf u}\norm{\mathbf v}$, since $|\cos\theta|\le1$. This bound explains the legal range of a computed cosine.

## Worked examples

For $\mathbf u=(3,0)$ and $\mathbf v=(2,2)$, the product is $6$. Their lengths are $3$ and $2\sqrt2$, so $\cos\theta=1/\sqrt2$ and $\theta=\pi/4$. Inspect the arithmetic before the angular interpretation.

## Common mistakes

Multiplying component sums, $(u_x+u_y)(v_x+v_y)$, introduces cross terms and is not a dot product. Treating zero product as proof of a right angle overlooks zero vectors. A large positive product also does not necessarily mean a smaller angle than a smaller product: different vector lengths can dominate the comparison. Normalized alignment removes that size dependence.

## Numerical considerations

Computing $\arccos((\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)/(\norm{\mathbf u}\norm{\mathbf v}))$ requires two nonzero vectors and finite, valid arithmetic. Normalizing after rescaling avoids intermediate squared-norm overflow. A rounded cosine can land a few ulps outside $[-1,1]$. The code allows at most eight binary64 epsilon units beyond an endpoint, reflecting the rounding of the normalized products and their sum. Values such as $1.4$, infinity, or NaN are rejected; unrestricted clipping would hide an invalid computation.

Near parallel directions, $\arccos$ is sensitive because cosine changes very slowly with angle. The stage retains an exact decimal cross product $c=u_xv_y-u_yv_x$ and evaluates the equivalent smaller angle $\operatorname{atan2}(|c|,\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)$. This preserves a small angle that might be lost by rounding cosine to $1$. Exact decimal orthogonality in the stage uses no tolerance; measured floating-point vectors require a comparison scaled to their magnitudes and measurement uncertainty.

## Engineering and ML connections

Work done by a force along a displacement is a dot product: perpendicular force contributes no work along that move. A weighted feature score also multiplies matching coordinates and sums them. Cosine similarity compares normalized alignment, so it is undefined for a zero input unless an application declares a separate convention. Two-dimensional geometry supplies the mental model; the matching-component sum extends to longer vectors.
