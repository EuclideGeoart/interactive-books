## Intuition: the accumulator's speedometer

Imagine a tank that fills at rate $f(t)$ (negative means draining). The amount in the tank since time $a$ is $F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$. How fast is the amount changing at time $x$? Exactly at the current rate, $f(x)$. Integration accumulates a rate; differentiation recovers it.

## Definition

For $f$ integrable on an interval containing $a$, the **accumulation function** is

$$
F(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt .
$$

**Fundamental theorem (first form).** If $f$ is continuous at $x$, then $F$ is differentiable at $x$ and $F'(x)=f(x)$.

**Second form.** If $G$ is any antiderivative of a continuous $f$ ($G'=f$), then $\int_a^b f=G(b)-G(a)$.

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

The integrands are piecewise polynomials, so $F$ is computed exactly as a piecewise polynomial that is continuous by construction. The strip's area $\Delta F=F(x+\Delta x)-F(x)$ is exact, as is the rectangle estimate $f(x)\,\Delta x$. The slope row is not $f(x)$ copied over: it is the derivative of $F$'s own exact pieces, including one-sided slopes at piece boundaries. Agreement with $f(x)$ is what the theorem predicts — and the jump preset shows where that agreement fails.

## Derivation: why F′ = f

For $\Delta x>0$,

$$
\frac{F(x+\Delta x)-F(x)}{\Delta x}=\frac1{\Delta x}\int_x^{x+\Delta x}f(t)\,dt ,
$$

the **average** of $f$ over the strip. If $f$ is continuous at $x$, every value of $f$ on a short enough strip is within any chosen tolerance of $f(x)$, so the average is too. Letting $\Delta x\to0$ (from either side) gives $F'(x)=f(x)$. If $f$ jumps at $x$, the averages from the left and right approach the two different one-sided values of $f$, and $F$ has a corner.

## Worked example

Take $f(t)=t^3/4-t$ and $a=-2$. Then

$$
F(x)=\frac{x^4}{16}-\frac{x^2}{2}+1 ,
$$

so $F(-2)=0$, $F(1)=9/16$ and $F(2)=0$. At $x=2$, $f$ changes from negative to positive, and $F$ has a local minimum; at $x=0$ it changes from positive to negative and $F$ has a local maximum $F(0)=1$. With $\Delta x=1/2$ at $x=1$, $\Delta F=F(1.5)-F(1)=-95/256$ while $f(1)\,\Delta x=-3/8=-96/256$.

## Sign changes, moving limits and jumps

* Where $f>0$, $F$ increases; where $f<0$, it decreases; where $f$ changes sign, $F$ has a local extremum.
* Moving $a$ shifts $F$ by a constant ($\int_{a'}^{a}f$). The slope $F'(x)=f(x)$ does not depend on $a$.
* With $x<a$, $F(x)=-\int_x^a f$: the accumulation runs backward.
* For a jump in $f$, $F$ is still continuous but has a corner; the first form of the theorem needs continuity at $x$.

## Common mistakes

Confusing $F(x)$ (an accumulated amount) with $f(x)$ (a rate). Thinking the lower limit affects $F'$. Applying $F'=f$ at a discontinuity of $f$. Forgetting that areas below the axis make $F$ decrease.

## Numerical considerations

Cumulative numerical integration followed by numerical differentiation recovers $f$ only approximately: discretisation error from the first step and the step-size trade-off of III.14 in the second. Working with exact antiderivatives, when they exist, avoids both.

## Engineering and ML connections

Cumulative quantities are everywhere: position from velocity, charge from current, a cumulative distribution function from a density, whose derivative recovers the density (IV.8). Prefix sums in computing are the discrete accumulation function, and their differences recover the original sequence.

The accumulation function $F(x)=\int_a^x f$ grows at the rate $f(x)$: $F'(x)=f(x)$ wherever $f$ is continuous. Changing $a$ shifts $F$ by a constant; a jump in $f$ makes a corner in $F$. Next, III.14 turns to computing derivatives numerically, where finite precision shapes the answer.
