## Intuition: repeat or reverse a move

The vector $(3,2)$ says “three right, two up.” Twice that move is $(6,4)$. Half that move is $(1.5,1)$. Scaling changes how much of the instruction you execute. A negative scalar first reverses the instruction and then changes its amount: $-2(3,2)=(-6,-4)$.

The scalar is one number. It acts on **both** components together. If you stretch only the horizontal component, the direction generally changes; that is a different operation. Uniform scalar multiplication keeps a nonzero vector on the same line through the origin.

## Definition

For $k\in\mathbb R$ and $\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^2$,

$$
k\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}kv_x\\kv_y\end{bmatrix}.
$$

If $k>0$ and $\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0$, $k\mathbf v$ points the same way. If $k<0$, it points the opposite way. If $k=0$ or $\mathbf v=\mathbf0$, the result is the zero vector. Its direction is undefined, regardless of the other input.

<Callout kind="misconception" title="Zero does not choose a direction">
At $k=0$, the arrow's length tends to zero but no direction survives in the zero vector itself. Approaching zero through positive scalars and negative scalars gives opposite directions. Assigning either direction to the result would add information the vector does not contain.
</Callout>

## Mechanics: sign and amount are separate

The scalar control accepts decimals between $-2$ and $2$ with at most two decimal places. This range is an exploration window, not a restriction on the mathematical definition. Values outside it are rejected with a message. Arrow keys on the original vector move its components; arrow keys in the scalar field step $k$. Both input paths update the same lesson state.

Watch the inspector's two lengths. A negative $k$ can make the result longer, but it cannot make the length negative. At zero, the length remains a defined number while the direction row explicitly becomes undefined. Making $\mathbf v=\mathbf0$ produces that same exception even if $k$ is positive.

## Derivation: the norm uses an absolute value

Apply the Euclidean length formula from <Xref to="chapters/vectors/">I.3 Vectors</Xref>:

$$
\norm{k\mathbf v}
=\sqrt{(kv_x)^2+(kv_y)^2}
=\sqrt{k^2(v_x^2+v_y^2)}
=|k|\norm{\mathbf v}.
$$

The last step uses $\sqrt{k^2}=|k|$, which holds for every real $k$. Writing $k$ instead would fail whenever $k$ is negative. Squared length scales by $k^2$; length scales by $|k|$.

The component definition also gives three useful laws:

$$
k(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)=k\mathbf u+k\mathbf v,\qquad
(a+b)\mathbf v=a\mathbf v+b\mathbf v,\qquad
a(b\mathbf v)=(ab)\mathbf v.
$$

For example, the first horizontal component is $k(u_x+v_x)=ku_x+kv_x$. The vertical component follows the same real-number distributive law. Scaling a combined move therefore agrees with scaling the two moves first and then adding them.

## Worked examples

Take $\mathbf v=(3,2)$ and $k=-1.5$. The output is $(-4.5,-3)$. The original length is $\sqrt{13}$ and the new length is $1.5\sqrt{13}$. Both component signs reverse, while the length factor is positive.

## Common mistakes

Multiplying only one component is an axis stretch. Adding $k$ to both components is a translation in component space. Neither implements $k\mathbf v$. Also distinguish “opposite” from “perpendicular”: multiplying by $-1$ changes the direction by a half-turn, not a quarter-turn. A negative length is never the explanation of a reversed arrow.

## Numerical considerations

The stage uses exact decimal rationals for multiplication and an exact radical when expressing the norm. Rendered screen coordinates and rounded norm readouts are numerical views of those values. In ordinary floating point, a very large product can overflow and a very small product can underflow to zero. Once an output has underflowed to zero, an algorithm must not claim its stored components still specify a unit direction. The conceptual norm law remains true over real numbers even when its direct numerical implementation loses information. Exact entered decimals also differ from exact representations of a binary64 measurement; choose the input interpretation deliberately.

## Engineering and ML connections

A gain multiplies a signal; a time interval scales a velocity into a displacement. Units must be tracked: seconds times metres per second produce metres. In parameter updates, a learning rate scales a direction before addition. Negative scaling reverses an update, so signs in an optimization formula determine whether the move follows or opposes a gradient. Later chapters will derive those directions rather than assume them.
