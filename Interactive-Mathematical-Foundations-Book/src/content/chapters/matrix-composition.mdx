## Intuition: replace a pipeline without erasing its order

Two transformations form a pipeline. $A$ consumes the original input; $B$ consumes the output of $A$. A matrix product packages that pipeline as one linear map, but the visible intermediate state explains what the package means.

Function notation already fixes the order:

$$
\mathbf x\longmapsto A\mathbf x\longmapsto B(A\mathbf x).
$$

The later operation appears on the left, so the combined matrix is $BA$.

## Definition

If $A$ is $m\times n$ and $B$ is $p\times m$, their product $BA$ is the $p\times n$ matrix whose entries are

$$
(BA)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}b_{ik}a_{kj}.
$$

Its $j$th column is $B$ applied to the $j$th column of $A$. Therefore $BA$ records where the complete composition sends every input basis vector.

## Mechanics: follow dimensions and the middle space

The dimension chain is

$$
\mathbb R^n\xrightarrow{A}\mathbb R^m\xrightarrow{B}\mathbb R^p,
\qquad
(p\times m)(m\times n)=p\times n.
$$

The shared $m$ is the intermediate space. A $3\times2$ map followed by a $2\times3$ map produces a $2\times2$ combined map. Reversing the factors describes a different route and yields a $3\times3$ matrix.

## Derivation: associativity connects the pipeline

Expand one output component:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bigl(B(A\mathbf x)\bigr)_i
&=\sum_k b_{ik}(A\mathbf x)_k\\
&=\sum_k b_{ik}\sum_j a_{kj}x_j\\
&=\sum_j\left(\sum_k b_{ik}a_{kj}\right)x_j\\
&=((BA)\mathbf x)_i.
\end{aligned}
$$

This proves $B(A\mathbf x)=(BA)\mathbf x$. Regrouping is valid, so matrix multiplication is associative. Swapping factors is a different operation, so multiplication is generally not commutative.

## Noncommutativity in geometry

Consider a horizontal shear and a quarter-turn. Shearing a shape and then turning it does not usually place its slanted edge where turning first and shearing does. The stage draws both $BA$ and $AB$ exactly; unequal results are structural, not a rendering artifact.

Some particular matrices do commute, including every matrix with the identity. One agreeing example does not establish a general commutative law.

## Affine composition

Suppose the first map is $\mathbf x\mapsto A\mathbf x+\mathbf b$ and the second is $\mathbf y\mapsto B\mathbf y+\mathbf c$. Substitution gives

$$
B(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b)+\mathbf c
=(BA)\mathbf x+(B\mathbf b+\mathbf c).
$$

The linear parts multiply in the same order. The first translation is itself transformed by $B$ before the second translation is added. Simply adding $\mathbf b+\mathbf c$ is generally wrong.

## Worked examples

For

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix},
&B&=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix},\\
\mathbf x&=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

we obtain $A\mathbf x=(3,1)$, then $B(A\mathbf x)=(3,4)$. Direct multiplication gives $BA=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, and $(BA)\mathbf x=(3,4)$.

## Common mistakes

Do not read $BA$ left to right as a timeline. Do not multiply entries position by position. Do not assume $AB=BA$, and do not infer that both orders are even defined for rectangular matrices. For affine maps, do not fold translations into the linear product or add them without transforming the earlier shift.

## Numerical considerations

Exact rational examples make sequential and combined outputs identical. Floating-point software may produce slightly different last bits because the two evaluation orders group sums differently. In deep pipelines, shapes and operation order should be asserted explicitly, while performance libraries choose stable kernels appropriate to the hardware.

## Engineering and ML connections

Layers, coordinate changes, camera transforms, and data-processing stages compose in this same order. Several linear layers with no intervening nonlinearity collapse mathematically to one matrix; nonlinear activations prevent that collapse and give deep networks more expressive structure. Bias vectors follow the affine composition law above.
