## Intuition: three ways to read probability

A discrete mass function attaches probability to each possible value. A continuous density spreads probability across intervals as area. A cumulative distribution asks a common question for both models: how much probability lies at or below this threshold?

Linking the views makes the distinction visible. A discrete PMF bar becomes a CDF jump of the same size. A continuous density accumulates smoothly; its height controls how quickly the CDF grows, rather than assigning positive mass to a single point.

## Definition

For a discrete variable, the **PMF** is $p(x)=P(X=x)$, with nonnegative masses summing to one. For a variable with a **PDF** $f$, interval probability is the integral of a nonnegative density whose total area is one. The **CDF** of any real-valued random variable is

$$
F(x)=P(X\le x).
$$

It is nondecreasing and right-continuous, approaching $0$ and $1$ at the two infinite extremes. Let $F(a^-)=P(X<a)$ denote its left limit. Then $F(a)-F(a^-)=P(X=a)$; the difference measures a possible point mass.

## Mechanics: link bars or area to accumulation

The stage offers discrete, uniform, and normal modes. Move interval bounds and compare highlighted masses or density area with the CDF endpoints, inspector, equation, and trace. The default discrete mode is $\operatorname{Binomial}(2,1/2)$, with interval bounds $0$ and $1$ inclusive.

At a discrete jump, distinguish the value at the endpoint from its left limit. $F(1)=3/4$ includes the mass at $1$; $F(1^-)=1/4$ excludes it. Just to the right of $1$, the CDF remains $3/4$. Read endpoint markers and labels alongside the curve to recognize right-continuity.

## Derivation: choose the correct subtraction

Subtract all probability strictly below $a$ from all probability at or below $b$:

$$
P(a\le X\le b)=F(b)-F(a^-).
$$

Subtracting $F(a)$ instead gives $P(a<X\le b)$ and omits a possible lower-endpoint mass. For the continuous uniform and normal models, singleton probabilities vanish, so $F(a^-)=F(a)$ and the familiar area formula is $F(b)-F(a)$. Equal bounds thus give a discrete point mass but zero continuous area.

For a density, $F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$. Where the density is continuous, $F'(x)=f(x)$. A PMF is a jump size, while a PDF is a rate of accumulation; neither is generally the cumulative height.

## Worked examples

For $\operatorname{Binomial}(2,1/2)$, the masses at $0,1,2$ are $1/4,1/2,1/4$. Therefore $F(0)=1/4$, $F(1)=3/4$, and $F(2)=1$. Since $F(0^-)=0$,

$$
P(0\le X\le1)=\frac34-0=\frac34,
\qquad P(0<X\le1)=\frac34-\frac14=\frac12.
$$

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

The PMF and CDF at $1$ differ: $1/2$ is the jump size and $3/4$ is the cumulative height. Treating the CDF as left-continuous would assign its lower jump height at a support point and change the event from $X\le x$ to $X<x$.

For a continuous PDF, $f(0)>0$ does not imply $P(X=0)>0$. A point has no interval width. Also, not every distribution has a PDF: the discrete model uses masses, and mixed distributions require both continuous and point-mass contributions.

## Numerical considerations

Finite rational masses and their CDF sums can be exact. The NumPy reference checks the same finite mechanics in floating-point form. Normal areas are approximate: the model integrates a positive tail with composite Simpson's rule using 2048 subdivisions, truncated at $8$ standard deviations. Beyond that threshold the omitted one-sided tail is below $6.4\times10^{-16}$.

The absolute CDF error budget is $10^{-9}$, validated against an independent `math.erfc` calculation; subtracting two CDF values gives an absolute interval budget of $2\times10^{-9}$. The Gaussian drawing uses a dense grid within $\mu\pm4\sigma$ and extra interval and viewport points. Its polygonal area is not claimed to be exact or normalized. Drawing samples and numerical integration limits are separate choices.

<Callout kind="numerical" title="A small absolute error can be a large relative tail error">
The interval budget bounds absolute error, not relative accuracy for arbitrarily tiny tail probabilities. Do not interpret an approximately zero tail display as a proof that the Gaussian has finite support.
</Callout>

## Engineering and ML connections

Categorical predictions use PMF-like probability vectors. Continuous likelihoods are density values and depend on units. CDFs support threshold queries and interval checks across both types of variable. When writing an API or evaluation rule, specify the inequality at each endpoint so a point mass is not silently included or excluded.
