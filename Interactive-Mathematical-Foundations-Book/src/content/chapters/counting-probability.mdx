## Intuition: count what the experiment distinguishes

Giving a first prize to label 1 and a second prize to label 2 differs from giving the prizes in the reverse order. Choosing a two-person team from those labels ignores that order. The same labels therefore support different outcome spaces depending on the experiment's question.

Replacement asks a separate question: can a label be used again? Keeping these two choices separate prevents a correct counting formula from being used for the wrong experiment.

## Definition

Let $n$ be the number of distinct labels and $k$ the number selected, both integers. For ordered selection **without replacement**, there are

$$
n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}
$$

outcomes when $0\le k\le n$. Ignoring order gives the **combination count** $\binom nk=n!/(k!(n-k)!)$. The factorial convention is $0!=1$.

With replacement, ordered sequences number $n^k$. Unordered selections are **multisets**, which record how many times each label appears; for $n\ge1$ they number $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$. These are counts, not automatically a uniform probability model.

## Derivation: multiplication and grouping

Without replacement, the first slot has $n$ choices, the next has $n-1$, and so on. Multiply because each partial selection branches into that many next choices. Every unordered set of $k$ distinct labels has exactly $k!$ orderings. Dividing the ordered count by $k!$ therefore gives combinations.

With replacement, every slot has $n$ choices, giving $n^k$. To count unordered multisets, distribute $k$ indistinguishable marks among $n$ labelled boxes. A row of $k$ marks and $n-1$ separators encodes the counts. Choosing the mark positions gives $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$.

Repeated labels make the number of orderings vary by multiset. If a multiset has counts $c_1,\ldots,c_n$ summing to $k$, its number of ordered preimages is $k!/(c_1!\cdots c_n!)$. Under independent uniform draws its probability is that number divided by $n^k$; this is why “ignore order” does not automatically mean “make all unordered results uniform.”

## Worked examples

For $n=4$, $k=2$, without replacement, the ordered total is $4\times3=12$. Each pair occurs in two orders, so there are $12/2=6$ unordered pairs. The event “select labels 1 and 2” has probability $2/12=1/6$ under uniform ordered draws; equivalently it is one of six equally likely unordered pairs.

With replacement, there are $4^2=16$ sequences and $\binom52=10$ multisets. Four multisets repeat a label; six contain distinct labels. The result $\{1,1\}$ has one preimage and mass $1/16$. The result $\{1,2\}$ has two preimages and mass $1/8$. Assigning $1/10$ to each describes a different experiment: direct uniform selection of multisets.

## Common mistakes and counterexamples

Dividing every ordered replacement count by $k!$ fails when labels repeat. For two-of-four it gives $16/2=8$, but there are ten multisets. Using $n^k$ without replacement also overcounts repeated-label sequences that the experiment excludes.

An impossible selection is not an empty selection. One has no outcome; the other has the single empty outcome. Do not evaluate a factorial with a negative argument to extend the without-replacement formula past $k=n$; report count zero and the unavailable sampling model.

## Numerical considerations

Counts are exact integers and probabilities are exact rational ratios in the standard-library reference. The bounded explorer permits enumeration, but $n^k$ grows quickly outside that range. A finite display must never be mistaken for an exhaustive list in a much larger experiment.

Python integers grow as needed. NumPy integer arrays have fixed-width types, so a larger counting implementation must consider overflow before arithmetic. Here the bounded counts fit exactly; division into floating-point masses is approximate, checked with absolute tolerance $10^{-12}$ and zero relative tolerance. The reference does not claim a rounded array sum is an exact proof of normalization.

## Engineering and ML connections

Sampling a minibatch without replacement differs from independently choosing training examples with replacement. Sequence order matters for temporal input, whereas a selected subset may ignore it. Counting helps estimate search-space size and memory requirements, but probabilities still depend on the sampling rule. Grouping randomized outputs can introduce unequal category probabilities even when the underlying draws are uniform.
