## Intuition: rate times time, piece by piece

If a speed $f(t)$ were constant, distance would be speed × time. When it changes, chop time into short intervals on which it is nearly constant, use one sampled speed per interval, and add the products. A negative speed (moving backward) subtracts distance. The total is a **signed accumulation**; refining the chopping makes it exact in the limit.

## Definition

Partition $[a,b]$ into $n$ panels of width $\Delta x=(b-a)/n$ with points $x_i=a+i\,\Delta x$. A **Riemann sum** chooses a sample $\xi_i$ in each panel:

$$
S_n=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(\xi_i)\,\Delta x .
$$

The left, right and midpoint sums use $\xi_i=x_i$, $x_{i+1}$ or the centre; the **trapezoid sum** averages the two end heights. For continuous $f$, all of them converge to the same limit, the **definite integral**

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}S_n .
$$

By the same formula, reversing the limits reverses the sign of $\Delta x$: $\int_b^a f=-\int_a^b f$, and $\int_a^a f=0$.

## Mechanics: what the explorer computes

For the polynomial integrands every sum is computed exactly with rational arithmetic, and the integral comes from an exact antiderivative; their difference is the exact error. The positive part, the negative part and the geometric area $\int|f|$ are obtained by splitting at the exact rational roots. For $\sin x$ the values are binary64 and marked $\approx$. Each drawn panel is the panel used in the sum, with the same height and width.

## Derivation: signed area and geometric area

Split $[a,b]$ at the points where $f$ changes sign. On a subinterval where $f\ge0$, the integral is the area under the graph; where $f\le0$, it is minus the area above the graph. Hence

$$
\int_a^b f=\text{(area above)}-\text{(area below)},
$$

$$
\int_a^b|f|=\text{(area above)}+\text{(area below)}.
$$

For $x^3/4-x$ on $[-2,3]$ the roots are $-2$, $0$ and $2$: the area above is $1+25/16=41/16$, the area below is $1$, so the integral is $25/16$ and the geometric area $57/16$.

## Worked example

$f(x)=x^3/4-x$ on $[-2,3]$ with $n=10$ left panels: $\Delta x=1/2$, samples $-2,-1.5,\dots,2.5$. The exact sum is $45/64$. The right sum is $165/64$, the midpoint sum $195/128$ and the trapezoid sum $105/64$. With $n=64$ the midpoint and trapezoid sums are within $0.002$ of $25/16$, while the left and right sums are still off by about $0.15$: the sample point matters for the rate.

## Exceptional cases

* **Exact cancellation.** For $x-1$ on $[0,2]$ the integral is exactly $0$ although the area is $1$.
* **Reversed limits.** With $b<a$, every $\Delta x$ is negative and the sum changes sign.
* **Non-integrable behaviour.** A function with a pole inside the interval, such as $1/x$ on $[-1,1]$, has no Riemann integral at all; sample points can land arbitrarily close to the pole and the sums do not settle. The explorer's integrands are bounded on the whole window.

## Common mistakes

Treating the integral as area when the graph dips below the axis. Forgetting that $\Delta x$ is negative when $b<a$. Using $n$ panels but $n+1$ heights in a left or right sum (the trapezoid uses $n+1$ points, weighted).

## Numerical considerations

Floating-point sums of many small terms accumulate rounding error; summing in exact arithmetic avoids it, and in binary64 the order of summation (or compensated summation) matters for very large $n$. Riemann sums converge slowly — the left and right rules have error proportional to $\Delta x$ — which is why III.15 studies better rules.

## Engineering and ML connections

Integrals compute totals from rates: energy from power, distance from velocity, probability from a density (Part IV), expected values as weighted sums. Discretised integrals appear in every simulation and in numerical estimates of areas under ROC curves.

## Summary and checkpoints

A Riemann sum multiplies sampled heights by panel widths and adds; its limit is the definite integral, a signed accumulation. Area above the axis adds, area below subtracts; reversing the limits reverses the sign. Next, III.13 lets the upper limit move and discovers that the rate of accumulation is the integrand itself.
